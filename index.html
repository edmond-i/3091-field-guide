  2. Bonding & Solids — how atoms stick together (ionic, covalent, metallic).

01

Atoms & the Bohr Model

L01–L03 · Witt LN1

Before anything else, you need a working picture of what an atom actually is and where that picture came from. This unit builds it from first principles — no chemistry background assumed.

What's inside an atom

Every atom is built from three types of particle. Two of them — protons and neutrons — are clumped together in a tiny, dense center called the nucleus. The third — electrons — move around that nucleus in a cloud that's enormous by comparison.

Sense of scale

An atom is about 10⁻¹⁰ m across; its nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁵ m across — the nucleus is roughly 1/100,000th the size of the whole atom. If the atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a grape in the middle of the field. Almost everything you'd call the "size" of an atom is empty space occupied by the electron cloud.

Three numbers describe any atom:

  • Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. This is what defines which element it is — change Z, change the element.
  • Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons (A = Z + N).
  • In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons. An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons, so its electron count no longer matches Z — a positive ion (cation) has lost electrons, a negative ion (anion) has gained them.

A history in five models

Nobody has ever "seen" an atom directly. Everything we know about its structure was inferred from experiments, and the model of the atom changed five times as better experiments came along. Knowing this sequence is itself testable, and it explains why we believe what we believe about atoms today.

Dalton (1803): atoms are solid, indestructible balls — the smallest unit of matter, like a billiard ball. All atoms of one element are identical; different elements combine in whole-number ratios.

Thomson (1904): discovered the electron. Since atoms are neutral overall, he proposed the "plum pudding" model — electrons are small negative specks embedded in a sphere of spread-out positive charge, like fruit in a pudding.

Rutherford (1911) — the gold foil experiment: this is the experiment that killed the plum pudding model, and it's worth understanding in detail because it's a classic example of reasoning from indirect evidence. Rutherford fired a beam of alpha particles (positively charged, heavy — essentially helium nuclei) at an extremely thin sheet of gold foil, with a detector screen wrapped around it.

  • If Thomson was right (charge spread out evenly), the alpha particles should sail straight through with at most tiny deflections — like a bowling ball thrown through a cloud of flour.
  • What actually happened: most particles did pass straight through — but a small fraction deflected at large angles, and a few bounced almost straight back.

Rutherford's own words capture how surprising this was: "It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15 inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." The only explanation: most of the atom really is empty space, but all of its positive charge (and nearly all of its mass) is concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus. A head-on alpha particle hits that concentrated charge and bounces back; most particles, aimed at the empty space around it, pass straight through.

Radiation used in this experiment

Alpha (α) particles are heavy and positively charged (stopped by a sheet of paper); beta (β) particles are electrons (stopped by ~0.5 cm of lead); gamma (γ) rays are uncharged high-energy light (pass through paper and several cm of lead). Penetrating power: α < β < γ.

Bohr (1913): fixed the obvious problem with Rutherford's model — classical physics says an orbiting (accelerating) electron should continuously radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus in a fraction of a second, which plainly doesn't happen. Bohr proposed that electrons can only occupy specific, fixed circular orbits, and don't radiate while sitting in one. This model is mathematically powerful (see the Bohr model section) but still treats electrons as little balls on fixed tracks.

Schrödinger (1926): replaced fixed orbits with probability clouds — regions of space where an electron is likely (but never certain) to be found. This is the model actually used today, and it's where quantum numbers and orbital shapes come from.

Also from Lecture 2: Millikan's oil-drop experiment

Millikan (1909) sprayed charged oil droplets between two charged plates and adjusted the voltage until a droplet hung perfectly suspended (electric force balancing gravity). Every droplet's measured charge turned out to be a whole-number multiple of one value — proving that electric charge is quantized (comes in discrete chunks) and pinning down the charge of a single electron, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C.

Mendeleev's prediction (1869) — why the periodic table is trusted

Mendeleev built the periodic table by arranging elements in order of mass and grouping by recurring properties — and famously left a gap where an undiscovered element should sit (under silicon), predicting its properties in advance as "eka-silicon." When germanium was discovered 16 years later, it matched almost exactly:

Isotopes & atomic mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z, same number of protons) with a different number of neutrons (different A). Chemically they behave almost identically, because chemistry is driven by electrons — but their masses, and sometimes their nuclear stability, differ.

Isotope notation looks like ⁠ᴬ_Z X (mass number top-left, atomic number bottom-left). A shortcut: for an element with essentially one dominant isotope, A ≈ the atomic mass you'd read off a periodic table. This shortcut breaks for any element with multiple common isotopes — which brings up the single most common calculation in this topic:

Worked example — weighted-average atomic mass

The periodic table's "atomic mass" for an element is a weighted average across its naturally occurring isotopes, weighted by how abundant each one is. Copper is 69.09% ⁶³Cu (62.9298 amu) and 30.92% ⁶⁵Cu (64.9278 amu):

(62.9298 × 0.6909) + (64.9278 × 0.3092) = 63.55 amu — matches the periodic table value for Cu.

This weighted-average idea is also exactly how you'd convert between a volume/count of something and a mass using density and molar mass — the kind of multi-step unit-conversion chain ("dimensional analysis") that shows up constantly in this course. The rule of thumb that makes these chains manageable: write every quantity with its units, and chain multiplication factors so that units cancel until only the ones you want are left. If you ever forget a formula, you can often reverse-engineer it from units alone (density in g/cm³ tells you density = mass ÷ volume).

Trap: converting cm³, m³, etc.

A linear conversion factor (say, 1 m = 100 cm) must be cubed when converting volumes: 1 m³ = (100 cm)³ = 10⁶ cm³, not 100 cm³. This is the single most common silent error in unit-conversion problems.

Light & energy basics

Before you can understand the Bohr model, you need the vocabulary of light, because the Bohr model is really a theory about why atoms absorb and emit specific colors of light.

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Two numbers describe any single color of light, and they trade off against each other:

Speed of light
c = νλ
c = speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s, constant)  ·  ν (nu) = frequency (waves per second, Hz)  ·  λ (lambda) = wavelength (distance between wave peaks, m)

Since c is fixed, high frequency always means short wavelength and vice versa. The full electromagnetic spectrum, from longest to shortest wavelength: radio waves → microwaves → infrared → visible light (≈400–700 nm) → ultraviolet → X-rays → gamma rays. Inside the visible band, wavelength is what we perceive as color: violet/blue ≈ 400–500 nm, green/yellow ≈ 500–590 nm, orange/red ≈ 590–700 nm.

The piece that makes quantum mechanics "quantum": light doesn't arrive in a continuous stream — it arrives in discrete packets called photons, each carrying a fixed amount of energy set only by its frequency.

Photon energy (Planck–Einstein relation)
E = hν = hc / λ
E = energy of one photon (J)  ·  h = Planck's constant = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s  ·  higher frequency / shorter wavelength = more energetic photon

Energies at the atomic scale are inconveniently small in joules, so chemists use a friendlier unit, the electron-volt (eV) — the energy gained by one electron crossing 1 volt of potential:

Unit conversion you'll use constantly
1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J
Worked example — the "photon energy chain"

A green laser pointer emits λ = 532 nm light. Find its energy in eV.

ν = c/λ = (2.998×10⁸ m/s)/(532×10⁻⁹ m) = 5.64×10¹⁴ Hz

E = hν = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(5.64×10¹⁴) = 3.74×10⁻¹⁹ J

E = 3.74×10⁻¹⁹ J ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 2.33 eV

This λ → ν → E → eV chain is, by far, the most frequently recurring calculation across every recitation and problem set you have — memorize the two formulas above and you can always get through it.

The Bohr model, in detail

Bohr's goal: explain hydrogen (the simplest atom — one proton, one electron) using five postulates:

  1. Rutherford's nuclear picture is correct (dense positive nucleus, electron around it).
  2. Classical electromagnetism does not apply to the orbiting electron — it doesn't radiate energy continuously.
  3. Ordinary Newtonian mechanics (forces, circular motion) does apply to describe the orbit itself.
  4. Electrons occupy only certain fixed circular orbits, indexed by a whole number n = 1, 2, 3, … (the principal quantum number) — never anything in between.
  5. An electron only radiates or absorbs energy when it jumps between orbits, and the energy of the photon involved exactly equals the energy gap between the two orbits: ΔE = hν.

The mathematical trick behind Bohr's model: an orbiting electron needs a centripetal force pulling it inward (supplied here by the electrical attraction between the negative electron and positive nucleus — Coulomb's law). Combine that force-balance with the postulate that angular momentum is quantized,

Quantized angular momentum
mvr = n(h/2π), n = 1, 2, 3, …
m = electron mass  ·  v = electron speed  ·  r = orbit radius

...and solving the algebra (full derivation in Witt's lecture notes, not required to memorize, but shown here once so the result doesn't feel like it fell from the sky) gives two clean results for hydrogen:

Orbit radius
rn = n² × a₀, a₀ = 0.529 Å (the "Bohr radius")
Orbits get dramatically bigger with n — radius scales as n², not n.
Orbit energy — the single most important equation in this unit
En = −13.6 eV / n² = −2.180×10⁻¹⁸ J / n²
Negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus — you'd have to put energy in to remove it. n=1 (ground state) is the most negative, i.e. most tightly bound. As n → ∞, E → 0 (electron is free, no longer part of the atom).

Notice the energy gaps shrink fast as n grows (1/n² scaling) — levels bunch up tightly near zero. Ionization energy (the energy to fully remove the electron, n=1 → n=∞) is just E∞ − E₁ = 0 − (−13.6 eV) = 13.6 eV, hydrogen's famous number.

This model generalizes directly to any hydrogen-like ion — any atom stripped down to a single electron, like He⁺ or Li²⁺ — by scaling with the square of the nuclear charge Z:

Hydrogen-like ions (one-electron species)
En = −13.6 eV × Z² / n²
Z = atomic number (nuclear charge). A more charged nucleus pulls the single electron in much harder — note it scales as Z², not Z.

Emission spectra — seeing the energy levels

Here's the payoff: when an electron falls from a higher orbit ni to a lower one nf, it emits a photon whose energy exactly equals the gap between those two levels. Because the levels are discrete, only specific photon energies (hence specific colors/wavelengths) come out — not a smooth rainbow. This is exactly what's observed when hydrogen gas is excited: a handful of sharp, bright lines, not a continuous spectrum.

Rydberg equation
1/λ = R · Z² · |1/nf² − 1/ni²|
R = Rydberg constant = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹  ·  same equation as ΔE above, just rearranged into 1/λ form.

Trap: sign conventions in the Rydberg equation

Wavelength must come out positive. For emission (electron falls, ni > nf), using the formula as written gives a positive result directly. For absorption (electron rises, ni < nf), either swap which level you call ni/nf, or just take the absolute value at the end. Emission and absorption are the same transition, same |ΔE|, opposite direction.

Quantum numbers: an electron's full address

Bohr's model only needed one number (n) because it only had to describe hydrogen's circular orbits. The real, 3D quantum-mechanical picture needs four numbers to fully specify where an electron lives and how it's spinning. Think of it like a postal address that gets more specific at each step: country → city → street → house number.

Worked example — the quantum-number validity check (very high-yield)

This is one of the most frequently repeated question types across your recitations. The rules: n must be a positive integer; 0 ≤ l ≤ n−1; −l ≤ ml ≤ +l; ms must be exactly +1/2 or −1/2.

  • [1, 0, 0, 0] — invalid (ms must be ±1/2, not 0)
  • [1, 1, 0, 1/2] — invalid (l must be < n; here l=1 is not allowed when n=1)
  • [2, 1, −1, −1/2] — valid
  • [2, 1, 2, −1/2] — invalid (ml must satisfy |ml| ≤ l; here l=1 so ml can only be −1, 0, +1)

Orbital shapes & nodes

An orbital is the actual 3D region of space where an electron has a high probability of being found — it is the physical, real-world meaning behind the quantum numbers above. A node is a region where that probability drops to exactly zero.

  • s orbitals are spheres. Higher-n s orbitals are bigger spheres with internal nested nodes (like an onion) — 1s has no interior node, 2s has one, 3s has two.
  • p orbitals are dumbbells — two lobes with a flat nodal plane of zero probability straight through the nucleus between them. There are three p orbitals per shell (px, py, pz), one pointing along each spatial axis.
  • d orbitals are mostly four-lobed "cloverleaf" shapes (one, dz², looks different — a dumbbell with a donut around its middle). There are five of them.
Degenerate orbitals

Orbitals that share the same energy are called degenerate. All three 2p orbitals are degenerate with each other; all five 3d orbitals are degenerate with each other. This matters directly for the filling rules in the next unit.

02

Building the Periodic Table: Multi-electron Atoms & Trends

L04–L06

Hydrogen has one electron, so there's nothing to argue about where it goes. Every other atom has multiple electrons competing for the lowest-energy orbitals — three simple rules settle the competition, and those rules are the actual reason the periodic table has the shape it does.

The three filling rules

1. Pauli Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in the same atom can share all four quantum numbers. Practically: each orbital (fixed n, l, ml) holds at most 2 electrons, and if it holds 2, they must have opposite spin (+1/2 and −1/2).

2. Aufbau ("building-up") Principle

Electrons fill the lowest-energy available orbitals first.

3. Hund's Rule

Within a set of degenerate orbitals (like the three 2p's), electrons fill each one singly, with parallel spin, before any orbital gets a second electron. (Think of it like passengers on a bus each taking their own double seat before anyone has to sit next to a stranger.)

Aufbau is easy to state but the actual energy order of orbitals is not simply 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s… — subshells from different shells interleave. The rule that predicts the correct order is the n+l rule:

The n+l rule

Fill orbitals in order of increasing (n + l). If two subshells tie on (n+l), the one with the smaller n fills first.

Worked example — why 4s fills before 3d

3d: n+l = 3+2 = 5     4s: n+l = 4+0 = 4

4s has the lower n+l, so it fills first — confirmed by real configurations: K = [Ar]4s¹, Ca = [Ar]4s², then Sc = [Ar]4s²3d¹. This single exception (4s before 3d, despite n=3 "feeling" like it should come first) is exactly why the d-block (transition metals) sits where it does on the table, two periods "behind."

Worked example — a three-way tie

3d: 3+2=5    4p: 4+1=5    5s: 5+0=5

All tie at n+l=5, so ascending n breaks the tie: 3d, then 4p, then 5s. The same logic, run out further, explains why the f-block (lanthanides) sits detached at the bottom of the table: 4f (n+l=7) ties with 5d, 6p, and 7s, but wins the tiebreak with the smallest n — so the lanthanide series fills 4f first.

The classic memorization aid for this order is the "diagonal rule" diagram — write subshells in rows by n (1s / 2s 2p / 3s 3p 3d / 4s 4p 4d 4f / …) and draw diagonal arrows through them top-to-bottom; reading off each arrow in sequence reproduces the n+l filling order automatically.

Writing electron configurations

An electron configuration is shorthand listing which subshells are occupied and by how many electrons, e.g. carbon = 1s²2s²2p² (2 in 1s, 2 in 2s, 2 in 2p — 6 total, matching carbon's atomic number). To save writing, you can replace the filled "core" with the symbol of the nearest smaller noble gas in brackets: Mg = 1s²2s²2p⁶3s² = [Ne]3s².

The skill the course actually wants

You are explicitly expected to derive a configuration from an element's position on the periodic table (which block it's in, how far across that block), not memorize or copy it off a reference table. Practice reading the table as a map of s/p/d/f blocks: Groups 1–2 fill s; Groups 13–18 fill p; the transition metals fill d (one period "behind"); the lanthanides/actinides fill f (two periods "behind").

Ions: for a cation, remove electrons from the atom's outermost shell first — note this is not always the orbital that was filled last. Transition metals are the classic trap: even though 4s filled before 3d going in, 4s electrons are always removed before 3d when forming a cation, because once 3d starts filling, it drops below 4s in energy — 4s becomes the "outermost," easiest-to-remove shell.

A handful of elements break the naive n+l order because a half-filled or completely filled d-subshell is unusually stable (electrons spread across degenerate orbitals repel each other less).

Mo and Ag/Au follow the same pattern one row down (4d and 5d).

Measuring the unseen: ionization energy & PES

Nobody can directly "see" an electron's energy level. It has to be inferred by knocking the electron out and measuring how much energy that took.

Ionization energy

The energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom. The first ionization energy removes the outermost (easiest) electron; the second removes the next one from the resulting cation, and so on. Successive ionization energies always increase — you're always pulling an electron away from a more positively-charged (and therefore more attractive) ion than before.

Worked example — the big jump that reveals shell structure

Sodium's successive ionization energies (kJ/mol): I₁ = 495.8, I₂ = 4562.4.

Na is [Ne]3s¹ — only one valence electron. Removing it (I₁) is relatively easy. Removing the next electron (I₂) means reaching into the full, tightly-bound inner [Ne] shell — nearly a 9× jump. This single huge jump is direct experimental proof that electrons really are organized into separate shells, not just spread out uniformly.

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) is the main technique used to measure these energies directly, and reading a PES spectrum is a signature 3.091 skill.

The PES energy balance
Ebinding = Eincident photon − Eejected electron's kinetic energy
Shine a photon of known energy at a sample; measure the kinetic energy of the electron it knocks out; the difference is exactly how tightly that electron was bound.

A PES spectrum plots a peak for every distinct orbital in the atom — peak position (binding energy) tells you how tightly that electron is held; peak height/area tells you how many electrons are in that orbital (so a filled s-orbital's peak is twice the height of a half-filled p-orbital holding one electron).

Worked example — identifying an unknown from its spectrum

A PES spectrum for a 3+ ion shows four peaks (highest to lowest binding energy) in a 2 : 2 : 6 : 1 height ratio.

Reasoning: the heights give the configuration directly — 1s², 2s², 2p⁶, then a half-height peak = 3s¹. Total electrons = 2+2+6+1 = 11. The ion is 3+, so it has 3 more protons than electrons: Z = 14 → Silicon, as Si³⁺.

Trap to notice: neutral sodium (Z=11, 11 electrons: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹) has the exact same peak pattern/heights as Si³⁺ — both have 11 electrons in the same configuration (they're isoelectronic). The difference: Si has more protons pulling on those same 11 electrons, so every one of its peaks sits at a higher binding energy than sodium's. Pattern tells you the configuration; position tells you the identity.

The big four periodic trends

Every trend on the periodic table reduces to a tug-of-war between two effects, and once you internalize these two, you can re-derive (rather than memorize) every trend direction:

  • Nuclear charge (more protons = stronger pull): moving across a period (left to right), protons increase while electrons are added to the same shell — more pull on the same-distance electrons.
  • Shielding / distance (more inner shells = weaker pull): moving down a group, a whole new outer shell is added, farther from the nucleus and partly screened from it by all the inner-shell electrons.
TrendAcross a period →Down a group ↓Why
Atomic radiusdecreasesincreasesmore protons pull the same shell in tighter; a new shell makes the atom bigger
1st ionization energyincreasesdecreasesharder to pull an electron away from a more tightly-held, less-shielded atom
Electron affinity (magnitude)increasesdecreasessame logic as IE — greatest in the p-block, especially halogens
Electronegativityincreasesdecreasesstronger pull on shared (bonding) electrons

Ionic radius footnote: a cation (lost electrons) is always smaller than its neutral parent atom; an anion (gained electrons) is always larger — removing electrons leaves fewer electrons for the same (or greater, per-electron) nuclear pull to squeeze in, while adding electrons increases electron–electron repulsion and expands the cloud.

Trap: the ionization-energy "zig-zag" exceptions

Ionization energy doesn't increase perfectly smoothly across a period — it dips twice, and both dips are explained by extra stability of certain configurations:

  • Be > B: removing B's single 2p electron is easier than removing one of Be's 2s electrons, because it leaves behind a extra-stable, completely full 2s² behind, and the 2p electron is slightly higher energy/more shielded to begin with.
  • N > O: N's 2p³ is half-filled (extra stable, per Hund's rule reasoning); removing an electron from O's 2p⁴ actually relieves electron-electron repulsion (two electrons forced to pair up in one 2p orbital) and restores that stable half-filled state — so it's easier than you'd expect.

Ionic Bonding & Ionic Solids

L07–L08

Atoms almost never exist on their own in nature — they bond, because bonded states are usually lower in energy (more stable) than separate atoms. This unit covers the first of two bonding types: ionic, where electrons are fully transferred from one atom to another.

The octet rule

Atoms tend toward having a full outer shell — for most elements, 8 valence electrons (an "octet"), matching the electron count of the nearest noble gas. This is the single biggest driving force behind how atoms gain, lose, or share electrons.

  • Metals generally have few valence electrons and achieve a full shell by losing them, becoming cations.
  • Non-metals generally have many valence electrons and achieve a full shell by gaining a few, becoming anions.
  • Metalloids can go either way depending on what they're bonding with.

A Lewis dot structure is a simple drawing of an atom's symbol surrounded by dots representing its valence electrons — dots are placed one per side before any side gets a second (mirroring Hund's rule). For example: potassium (K) has 1 valence electron → one dot. K⁺ has lost it → no dots, with a + charge. Nitride (N³⁻) has gained 3 electrons to reach 8 → four pairs of dots, with a 3− charge.

Free radicals

A species with an incomplete octet — typically from an odd total electron count, which makes a full octet on every atom impossible. Free radicals are unusually reactive (they "want" one more electron to complete their octet) and show up in biology (reactive oxygen species) and battery chemistry.

Why ions actually bond: the energy story

Transferring an electron from one atom to another isn't automatically a good deal energetically — it only pays off once you add up all three steps involved. Walk through the formation of LiF, atom by atom:

  1. Ionize lithium: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻. This costs energy (ionization energy): +520 kJ/mol.
  2. Attach the electron to fluorine: F + e⁻ → F⁻. This releases energy (electron affinity): −328 kJ/mol.
  3. Net so far: +520 − 328 = +192 kJ/mol — actually unfavorable! Just forming the separate ions costs more energy than it releases.
  4. Let the oppositely-charged ions attract each other (Coulombic/electrostatic attraction, pulling Li⁺ and F⁻ together): releases a large amount of energy, roughly −700 kJ/mol.

Add it up: +520 − 328 − 700 ≈ −508 kJ/mol — strongly favorable overall. The electrostatic attraction between the finished ions is what actually pays for the whole process — not the electron transfer itself. This is the key conceptual point of ionic bonding: ionization energy and electron affinity are almost always a losing trade on their own; Coulombic attraction between the resulting ions is what makes the whole thing worthwhile.

Why ionic compounds form solid crystals, not isolated pairs

Once a Li⁺ and F⁻ pair up, that pair doesn't stop attracting other ions — a nearby F⁻ is still pulled toward the Li⁺, and a nearby Li⁺ toward the F⁻. The attraction keeps extending outward in 3 dimensions, which is exactly why ionic compounds form extended crystal lattices (like table salt grains) rather than individual two-atom molecules the way, say, H₂ does.

The Born–Haber cycle

The LiF arithmetic above is a simplified version of a more general and more exam-relevant tool: the Born–Haber cycle. It's just conservation of energy — the enthalpy (heat) of forming a solid ionic compound directly from its elements must equal the sum of every intermediate step's enthalpy, no matter what path you take to get there.

Born–Haber cycle (general form, 1-electron-transfer case, e.g. NaCl)
ΔHf = V + IE + ½B + EA + UL
ΔHf = enthalpy of formation of the solid  ·  V = enthalpy to vaporize/sublime the solid metal into a gas  ·  IE = ionization energy of the metal  ·  B = bond-dissociation energy of the diatomic nonmetal gas (halved, since only 1 atom is needed per formula unit)  ·  EA = electron affinity of the nonmetal (usually negative/exothermic)  ·  UL = lattice energy (always the big exothermic/negative closing step)
Worked example — solving for lattice energy (NaCl)

Given: EA(Cl) = −355, IE(Na) = +502, V(Na) = +107, ΔHf(NaCl) = −411, B(Cl₂) = +242 (all kJ/mol).

−411 = 107 + 502 + ½(242) + (−355) + UL

−411 = 107 + 502 + 121 − 355 + UL = 375 + UL

UL = −411 − 375 = −786 kJ/mol

This is the signature calculation of the unit — expect either this simple 1-electron version, or a harder 2-electron-transfer version (e.g. CaO, where you need both the 1st and 2nd ionization energies of Ca, and both electron affinity steps of O). One subtlety worth internalizing: adding a second electron onto an already-negative ion (O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻) is actually energetically unfavorable on its own (you're forcing a negative charge onto something already negative) — the overall compound still forms because the much bigger lattice-energy payoff more than makes up for it.

Lattice energy & the Born–Landé equation

Lattice energy can't be measured directly (you can't "undo" a crystal into gas-phase ions in a lab and watch a meter). The Born–Haber cycle above gets it indirectly, from other measurable quantities. There's also a direct, geometric way to calculate it, built from pure electrostatics:

Born–Landé equation
E = − (NA · M · Z⁺ Z⁻ · e²)/(4πε₀ r₀) × (1 − 1/n)
NA = Avogadro's number, 6.022×10²³  ·  M = Madelung constant (depends on crystal structure)  ·  Z⁺, Z⁻ = ion charges  ·  r₀ = equilibrium distance between ion centers  ·  n = Born exponent (short-range repulsion term, 5–12)

Two terms here need their own definitions:

Madelung constant (M)

A single ion in a real crystal isn't just attracted to its immediate neighbor — it's attracted to (or repelled by) every other ion in the entire lattice, near and far. The Madelung constant is the infinite sum of all of those pairwise interactions, collapsed into one number. It depends only on the geometry of the crystal structure (rock salt vs. CsCl vs. fluorite, etc.) — not on which specific ions are involved.

Born exponent (n)

Captures how sharply the repulsive force ramps up as ions get too close (electron clouds overlapping). It depends on which noble gas the ion's electron configuration resembles: He-like → n=5, Ne-like → n=7, Ar-like → n=9, Kr-like → n=10, Xe-like → n=12 (use the average of the two ions' values for a compound).

Worked example — solving the Born–Landé equation for an unknown radius

Find the ionic radius of Cs⁺, given: UL(CsCl) = 633 kJ/mol, M = 1.763, n = 10.7, r(Cl⁻) = 1.81 Å.

Rearrange for r₀, plug in (converting UL to J/mol and including NA):

r₀ = r(Cs⁺) + r(Cl⁻) = 3.50 Å

r(Cs⁺) = 3.50 − 1.81 = 1.69 Å

Because looking up the exact crystal structure (to get M precisely) is often impractical, a simplified shortcut called the Kapustinskii equation is sometimes used instead: fix n = 9 and approximate M ≈ 0.88 × (number of ions in the formula unit) — trading a little accuracy for not needing structural data at all.

Two trends fall straight out of this equation and are worth internalizing rather than memorizing as separate facts: lattice energy increases with higher ionic charge (Z⁺Z⁻ in the numerator) and increases as ions get smaller (r₀ in the denominator — closer ions attract more strongly). This is also why melting point tracks lattice energy so closely: both come from the same underlying Coulombic attraction holding the solid together.

Once ions are set loose to pack together, geometry takes over: how many anions can physically fit snugly around one cation (and vice versa) depends mostly on the ratio of their radii. This single ratio predicts which of a handful of standard structures a compound will adopt.

Properties of ionic solids

Every characteristic property of an ionic solid traces back to one fact: it's held together by strong, non-directional electrostatic attraction spread through the whole lattice.

  • High melting/boiling points — you have to overcome the full lattice energy to break the solid apart.
  • Soluble in polar solvents like water, which can surround and stabilize individual ions.
  • Poor electrical conductor as a solid (ions are locked in place, can't move to carry current) but a good conductor molten or dissolved (ions are now free to move).
  • Brittle — a shearing force shifts one plane of ions relative to another, lining up same-charge ions face to face; the sudden repulsion shatters the crystal cleanly along a flat plane, rather than deforming gradually like a metal.
  • Often transparent or vividly colored, depending on which ions are present and how they absorb light.

04

Covalent Bonding

L09

Ionic bonding is a full transfer of electrons. Covalent bonding is the other major strategy: two atoms share electrons instead, each gaining partial access to a filled outer shell without either one fully giving an electron away.

Electronegativity & classifying bond type

Electronegativity (χ, the Pauling scale) measures how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons in a bond — think of it as how hard an atom "tugs" in an electron tug-of-war. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (χ = 3.98); cesium and francium are among the least (χ ≈ 0.7–0.8).

The difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms, Δχ, predicts what kind of bond forms:

ΔχBond characterWhat's happening
< 0.4Pure (nonpolar) covalentelectrons shared almost perfectly evenly
0.4–1.8Polar covalentshared, but pulled noticeably toward the more electronegative atom
> 1.8Ionicpull is so lopsided it's effectively a full transfer

This also explains a fact worth internalizing: a pure element can never bond ionically with itself. Ionic bonding needs an electronegativity difference to create a cation/anion pair — two atoms of the same element have identical electronegativity (Δχ = 0), so the only options left are covalent (e.g. diamond, pure silicon) or metallic (e.g. iron, copper) bonding.

The bonding curve, with real numbers (H₂)

Covalent bonds follow the exact same shape of potential-energy-vs-distance curve introduced in Unit 3 for ionic bonds — only now the attraction comes from orbital overlap/electron sharing rather than Coulombic ion attraction. For H₂, the actual measured numbers are:

Interatomic distanceWhat's happening
r = 45 pmNuclei forced too close — strong repulsion, very high PE

The well depth at r₀ (432 kJ/mol here) is called the bond dissociation energy — exactly how much energy it takes to pull the bonded atoms fully apart, and a direct measure of how strong the bond is.

What a covalent bond physically is

When two atomic orbitals (say, two separate hydrogen 1s clouds) get close enough to overlap, that overlap creates a new, lower-energy shared region — an electron-pair bond, with two electrons of opposite spin occupying the combined space between both nuclei. The energy drop from forming this shared, overlapping cloud is the bond energy.

Drawing Lewis structures: the full method

This is the single most practiced skill in the covalent-bonding unit. Follow these steps in order, every time:

  1. Arrange the atoms. Put the least electronegative atom in the center (with the exception that hydrogen is always terminal/on the outside, never central — H can only form one bond).
  2. Count total valence electrons. Add up each atom's valence-electron count; for a polyatomic ion, add one electron per unit of negative charge, or subtract one per unit of positive charge.
  3. Check for an odd total. An odd electron count means the octet rule cannot be satisfied everywhere — you have a free radical with an incomplete octet, by necessity.
  4. Draw the skeleton, connecting arranged atoms with single bonds (2 electrons each).
  5. Distribute remaining electrons as lone pairs, filling outer (terminal) atoms' octets first.
  6. Check the electron count against the central atom. Leftover electrons after every terminal octet is filled go on the central atom — even past 8, if needed (a hypervalent/expanded octet, only possible for period-3-and-below elements, which have accessible d-orbitals).
  7. If any atom is still short of an octet, convert a lone pair on a neighboring atom into an additional bond (a double or triple bond) to that atom, and recheck.
Worked example — HCN, built step by step

Valence electrons: H(1) + C(4) + N(5) = 10. H is terminal; C is less electronegative than N, so C is central: H–C–N skeleton.

A single-bond skeleton leaves C with an incomplete octet and bad formal charges. Moving lone pairs from N to form a triple bond between C and N fixes it:

H−C≡N: — formal charge 0 on every atom, full octets on C and N, H has its 1 bond. No resonance structures are possible here.

Quick sanity-check patterns

Nitrogen usually forms 3 bonds (1 lone pair); oxygen usually forms 2 bonds (2 lone pairs); halogens usually form 1 bond (3 lone pairs). If your structure doesn't match these defaults, double-check it with formal charges before trusting it.

Formal charge vs. oxidation state

These sound similar but use opposite electron-counting conventions — mixing them up is a common and easy mistake.

Formal charge
FC = (valence e−) − (lone-pair e−) − (bonding e− ÷ 2)
Convention: every bonding pair is split evenly, 50/50, between the two bonded atoms, regardless of electronegativity. A molecule's formal charges must sum to its overall charge.
Oxidation state

The opposite convention: every bonding pair is assigned entirely to the more electronegative atom (as if the bond were fully ionic, even when it isn't). This is why, for water, formal charge on O is 0, but oxidation state of O is −2 — same molecule, two different bookkeeping conventions answering two different questions.

When choosing between multiple valid Lewis structures, two tiebreaker rules apply: (1) prefer the structure with formal charges closest to zero (minimize both magnitude and the number of atoms carrying a nonzero formal charge), and (2) if a negative formal charge is unavoidable, it should sit on the more electronegative atom.

Resonance & hypervalency

Resonance

Some molecules/ions have more than one equally valid Lewis structure, differing only in where a double bond is placed (e.g. NO₃⁻ has three equivalent structures, each with the double bond on a different oxygen). The real structure is a blend — a resonance hybrid — of all of them simultaneously, not any single drawn version.

Hypervalency (expanded octet)

Elements in period 3 or below (P, S, Cl, and beyond) have accessible d-orbitals and can hold more than 8 valence electrons around the central atom — e.g. PF₅ (10 electrons around P) or SF₆ (12 around S). Period 2 elements (C, N, O, F) can never do this — they have no d-orbitals available.

A related, more advanced idea mentioned in recitation: aromaticity — rings (like benzene) with extreme, fully delocalized resonance, stabilized when they have 4n+2 π-electrons (Hückel's rule: 6, 10, 14…).

05

Covalent Compounds & Molecular Shape

L10–L11

Unit 04 showed how to draw a molecule as dots and lines. Now two bigger questions: how do covalent substances behave (do they melt, dissolve, conduct?), and what 3D shape does a molecule actually take — because shape decides whether the molecule is polar, and polarity decides a huge amount of its behavior.

Ionic vs. covalent: how they behave

Both kinds of solid are held together by electrical attraction, but covalent substances come in two very different families, and mixing them up is the number-one mistake in this topic.

  • Network (giant) covalent solids — every atom is covalently bonded to its neighbors, all the way through the crystal, so the whole piece is effectively one giant molecule. Examples: diamond, quartz/silica (SiO₂).
  • Molecular covalent substances — made of separate, small molecules (water, sugar, CO₂, S₈). Covalent bonds hold each molecule together inside, but only weak attractions hold one molecule to the next.
Intra vs. inter (the lecture italicizes “inter” on purpose)

Intramolecular = inside one molecule: the strong covalent bonds. Intermolecular = between molecules: weak forces. When a molecular substance melts, boils, or dissolves, only the weak between-molecule forces are overcome — the covalent bonds inside each molecule stay intact. (Melting ice does not break H–O bonds.) The three kinds of weak force between molecules are the subject of Unit 07.

Picture it

A network solid is a single welded steel frame: to melt it you have to cut through the metal itself. A molecular solid is a pile of separate toy cars: to “melt” the pile you only have to shake the cars loose from each other — nobody has to un-weld a car.

Why ionic crystals are brittle (from the L10 hammer picture): one blow slides a layer by a single ion position, which lines up like charges face to face. They repel, and the crystal splits — and once one bond goes, they all go.

Ionic vs. covalent is a dial, not a switch

In Unit 04 you sorted bonds into three bins using the electronegativity difference Δχ. In reality bonding slides smoothly from covalent to ionic, and L10 gives the formula that shows it:

Percent ionic character (L10)
% ionic = 100 × [ 1 − exp( −Δχ² / 4 ) ]
Δχ = difference in electronegativity (look the values up on your periodic table — the slide says “Check your periodic table. There is a trend table.”). exp(x) = ex, the exponential button on your calculator.
Worked example — how ionic is H–Cl?

χ(Cl) − χ(H) = 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96

1 − exp(−0.96²/4) = 1 − exp(−0.2304) = 1 − 0.794 = 0.206

≈ 21% ionic — mostly covalent but clearly polarized (the lecture's plot puts HCl near 18%). The 0.4 and 1.8 cut-offs from Unit 04 are rules of thumb: at Δχ = 1.8 the formula already gives 55%.

Same atoms, many structures: SiO₂ and sulfur

Two examples from L10 hammer home that structure, not just composition, decides properties.

  • SiO₂ (silica) — χ(O) − χ(Si) ≈ 1.5, so the Si–O bond is “mostly covalent but quite polarized”. The same formula appears as dense crystals (quartz, tridymite, cristobalite), nanoporous crystals (zeolites, full of tiny channels), and amorphous glass: fused silica (“quartz glass”, transparent even to UV) and fumed silica (a fine powder).
  • Sulfur — the S₈ crown-shaped ring is the common form (plus 3 polymorphs); other rings Sn exist for n = 6, 7, 9–15, 18, 20; very long chains exist too; and at high temperature you get small gas molecules S₂ and S₃. The S₃ molecule is bent, with an angle of 117.36° — a preview of the VSEPR section below.
Two words the slides use without defining

Allotropes = different structural forms of the same element (diamond vs. graphite; S₈ rings vs. sulfur chains). Polymorphs = different crystal arrangements of the same substance (quartz vs. cristobalite). (Standard definitions — extra, not worded on the slides.)

VSEPR: predicting shape in four steps

VSEPR (say “vesper”) stands for Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion. The whole theory is one idea: electrons repel each other, so the electron clouds around a central atom spread as far apart as possible. Think of tying balloons together at one point — they automatically push apart into the most spread-out arrangement.

Cheat code · the VSEPR staircase
  1. Count electron groups on the central atom = (atoms attached) + (lone pairs on the central atom). A double or triple bond counts as one group — it points in one direction.
  2. Groups → parent shape (the balloon rule): 2 line · 3 flat triangle · 4 tetrahedron · 5 bipyramid · 6 octahedron.
  3. Each lone pair takes the place of one atom. Name the shape by the atoms only (look at the staircase below).
  4. Squeeze. Lone pairs and double bonds are fat; they push the neighbors closer together, so real angles come out a little under the ideal ones.

Then: all outer atoms identical and no lone pair on the center → the arrows cancel → nonpolar. Otherwise, usually polar.

Vocabulary (use the lecture's words). The lecture says “electron groups”; many books say “electron domains” or “steric number” — same thing. It labels shapes with the AXmEn code: A = the central atom, X = an atom bonded to it, E = a lone pair (the lecture draws it as a pair of dots). So AX₃E means “3 atoms and 1 lone pair”, and groups = m + n.

Step 1–2: how many groups, and what do they spread into?

Where do the angles come from? The groups sit on the surface of an imaginary sphere around the central atom and take the positions that keep them farthest apart. With five groups there are two kinds of position: the two axial spots (top and bottom) and the three equatorial spots (the belt around the middle), so you get two angles: 90° between an axial and an equatorial group, and 120° within the belt.

How to read the diagrams

Plain line = bond lying in the page. Solid wedge = bond coming toward you. Hashed wedge = bond going away from you. Teal lobe with two dots = lone pair. Dark balls = outer atoms; the teal ball = the central atom.

Why lone pairs matter so much

A bonding pair is stretched between two nuclei. A lone pair belongs to one nucleus, so its cloud sits closer in and is fatter. The lecture's wording: “Lone pairs are closer and take up more space” and they “push out” the other bonding pairs. The standard way to rank the pushing (extra, not worded on the slides):

Repulsion strength
lone pair–lone pair  >  lone pair–bonding pair  >  bonding pair–bonding pair
Lone pairs therefore claim the spots where they have the fewest close neighbors at 90°. That is why 5-group shapes put them equatorial (an equatorial spot has two 90° neighbors; an axial spot has three), and why the second lone pair in an octahedron sits opposite the first (180° apart, no 90° contact at all).

Shape decides polarity: add the arrows

A polar bond has a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end. Draw it as an arrow with a small cross on the positive end and the arrowhead on the negative end (the lecture's convention) — so the arrow points toward the more electronegative atom. A whole molecule's polarity is just all its bond arrows added as vectors (L11 slide 13). If they cancel, the molecule is nonpolar even though every bond in it is polar.

Cheat code · polar or nonpolar in 5 seconds
  • Zero-lone-pair column and all outer atoms identical → arrows cancel → nonpolar (CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, PF₅, SF₆).
  • A lone pair on the center, or different outer atoms (CHCl₃) → polar.
  • Extra (standard, not on the slides): two lone-pair shapes are secretly symmetric, so they also cancel — linear AX₂E₃ (XeF₂) and square planar AX₄E₂ (XeF₄).
Trap: polar bonds ≠ polar molecule

CO₂ has two very polar C=O bonds and is nonpolar overall. Always draw the shape first, then add the arrows.

What VSEPR can’t tell you

The lecture opens with an admission: “We have a great theory for molecular shape and dipole but it is missing some fundamental concepts.” Two gaps:

  • Bond order. Why is a double bond shorter and stronger than a single bond? VSEPR just counts a double bond as one group and moves on.
  • Orbitals. Atomic orbitals have fixed directions — the three p orbitals point along x, y, and z, exactly 90° apart — yet real molecules show 109.5° and 120°. Something must happen to the orbitals when atoms bond.

A quick reminder of the shapes involved (L12 slide 5): s orbitals are spheres (2s has one node, 3s two); the three p orbitals are dumbbells along x, y, z, each with a nodal plane through the nucleus; the five d orbitals are cloverleaf shapes with nodal planes (3dz² is a dumbbell with a ring around the middle).

Hybridization, built from scratch

The idea in one breath

Before an atom bonds, you blend its s and p orbitals (sometimes d too) into new, identical orbitals that point exactly where the bonds need to go. Nothing is created or destroyed: n orbitals in → n hybrid orbitals out. The rest of this section is just the story of why we bother, how to do the blending on paper, and how to read the result.

Five things this topic quietly assumes (tap a link if one feels shaky)
  1. An orbital is a region, not a track. It is where an electron is likely to be. s = a sphere. p = a dumbbell; there are three of them, pointing along x, y, z, at 90° to each other. → orbital shapes
  2. Electrons fill orbital “boxes” from the bottom, one per box before doubling up. A box diagram is just this rule drawn out. → filling rules
  3. A covalent bond is a shared pair, and usually each atom brings one unpaired electron. More unpaired electrons = more bonds possible. → Lewis structures
  4. A “group” is one atom or one lone pair around the center. VSEPR turned the group count into a shape. → VSEPR
  5. Orbitals are waves. Two waves in step make a bigger wave; out of step they cancel. That is all “mixing” will mean.

Part 1 · The puzzle hybridization was invented to solve

Take carbon. Its six electrons are 1s² 2s² 2p², but only the four outer (valence) ones matter for bonding. Draw them as boxes, the same way as in Unit 02:

The simple valence-bond rule is: a bond forms when two orbitals that each hold one electron overlap head-on and the two electrons pair up. So the number of bonds an atom can make = its number of unpaired electrons. Carbon has two. We would predict CH₂, with the two bonds pointing 90° apart (that is how far apart the two p orbitals are).

Reality: methane is CH₄ — four bonds, all identical, at 109.5°. No experiment can tell one C–H bond from another. Two facts fail at once:

Part 2 · The fix, in three moves

  1. Promote. Lift one electron from the 2s box into the empty 2p box. Now there are four unpaired electrons, so four bonds are possible. This costs a little energy (the 2p box sits higher), but each new bond releases energy, and two extra bonds repay the cost with interest. extra For carbon the promotion costs roughly as much as one C–H bond releases, so two additional bonds are a clear net win.
  2. Mix. Blend the one 2s orbital and the three 2p orbitals. The result is four brand-new orbitals, identical to each other, each with one electron, pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. Their energy sits between 2s and 2p. We call them sp³ (one s + three p).
  3. Bond. Each sp³ hybrid overlaps head-on with the 1s orbital of a hydrogen (also one electron). Each overlap is one σ bond. Four hybrids, four identical bonds, 109.5° apart: exactly what VSEPR said for four groups.
Common confusion: “is this really a sequence of events?”

No. The atom does not literally wait, promote, mix, then bond. Promote → mix → bond is simply the order you write it on paper so the bookkeeping works. It is a description, not a movie.

Part 3 · What does a hybrid orbital actually look like?

Orbitals are waves, and waves have a sign (the L12 slides colour it blue = + and red = −; it is the sign of the wave, not an electric charge). An s orbital is + everywhere. A p orbital is + on one side of the nucleus and − on the other. Add them:

  • On the side where the p is +, the s (+) adds to it: a big lobe.
  • On the side where the p is −, the s (+) partly cancels it: a small lobe.
  • The big lobe sticks out further, so it overlaps more with a neighbor’s orbital, which makes a stronger bond. That is the whole reason hybrids “point”.
  • More ingredients, same logic: 1 s + 2 p gives three hybrids 120° apart in a plane (sp²); 1 s + 3 p gives four hybrids 109.5° apart (sp³).

Part 5 · How to find the hybridization of any atom

Cheat code · the three-step recipe
  1. Draw the Lewis structure (Unit 04).
  2. Count the groups on the atom you care about = (atoms bonded to it) + (lone pairs on it). A double or triple bond to a neighbor still counts as one atom.
  3. Read off the hybrid: 2 → sp · 3 → sp² · 4 → sp³ · 5 → sp³d · 6 → sp³d².

Why don’t double bonds add to the count? Because only the first bond of a multiple bond uses hybrids (σ). The second and third use leftover, unmixed p orbitals (π).

Shortcut · count the π bonds

0 π bonds → sp³  ·  1 π bond → sp²  ·  2 π bonds → sp   (a double bond has one π, a triple bond has two).

It works because an sp atom keeps 2 unmixed p orbitals, sp² keeps 1, sp³ keeps 0 (3 p orbitals total minus the ones used). Valid for atoms with 4 or fewer groups.

Part 6 · Where do double and triple bonds come from?

Every bond between two atoms has exactly one σ bond (head-on overlap along the line between them). A second or third bond in the same pair of atoms is a π bond, built from p orbitals that were not used in the hybrids. Those leftover p orbitals stand perpendicular to the bond and overlap sideways.

Part 7 · Questions that trip people up

Do hybrid orbitals physically exist? model vs reality

They are a model. A lone carbon atom in space has a plain 2s and 2p; nobody observes sp³ orbitals directly. The hybrid description is a convenient way to describe the bonded atom, and it matches the shapes we measure. That is also why the lecture lists “is there evidence for electron promotions?” as a weakness.

What does the little “3” in sp³ mean? notation

It is a recipe, not a power: sp³ = one s plus three p. sp² = one s plus two p. sp = one s plus one p. No number on the s means exactly one. extra Percent s-character is then 1 ÷ (1 + number of p): sp = 50%, sp² = 33%, sp³ = 25%.

Where did the original s and p orbitals go? n in, n out

They were used up to make the hybrids. In sp³ all four are consumed. In sp² only two of the three p orbitals are used, and the third stays as an ordinary p — that is the one that becomes a π bond.

Do lone pairs get hybrids too? NH₃, H₂O

Yes. In NH₃ the nitrogen is sp³: three of the four hybrids make N–H bonds and the fourth holds the lone pair. That is why lone pairs count as groups in the recipe.

If NH₃ is sp³, why is its angle 107° and not 109.5°? label vs angle

The hybrid label only tells you how many groups there are, hence the parent shape. The exact angle then comes from the VSEPR “squeeze”: the lone pair is fatter than a bonding pair and presses the N–H bonds closer together.

Do atoms really use d orbitals in sp³d and sp³d²? expanded octets

For this course, yes: use sp³d and sp³d² exactly as the slides do (L12 slide 10 shows them as the valence-bond way to handle PCl₅ and SF₆). extra Be aware that many chemists now think d orbitals contribute very little in these molecules, and describe them with MO theory instead — which is one reason the lecture lists MO as the more complete theory.

Hybridization or molecular orbitals: which do I use? choosing a theory

Shape, and which orbitals make a given bond: VSEPR and hybridization. Bond order, unpaired electrons, odd-electron molecules, excited states: molecular orbitals. See the scoreboard.

The lecture’s own criticisms of valence bond theory

(1) “Why these combinations and not others?” (2) “Is there evidence for electron ‘promotions’?” Example: ground-state Be (2s²) has no unpaired electron to bond with, so the hybrid picture quietly assumes one 2s electron is “promoted” into a 2p orbital before mixing. It works, but it is a bookkeeping device, not something we directly observe.

Molecular orbitals: mixing orbitals from two atoms

MO theory drops the idea of “this bond belongs to this pair”. Instead, when two atoms come close, their atomic orbitals overlap and combine into new orbitals that belong to the whole molecule. The combining works like waves, because orbitals are waves:

  • In phase (add): constructive interference → electron density piles up between the nuclei, which holds them together → a bonding MO, lower in energy than the starting orbitals.
  • Out of phase (subtract): destructive interference → the wave cancels in the middle, leaving a node (zero density) between the nuclei → an antibonding MO (marked with an asterisk *), higher in energy.
“Phase is not charge” (lecture slide 8)

In every orbital picture below, blue and red mark the sign of the wave (+ or −), not positive or negative electric charge. The actual electron density is the wave squared (ψ²), which is never negative.

σ vs. π. The lecture picks z as the bond axis. Overlap head-on along that axis gives a σ orbital (density on the axis). Overlap sideways (px with px, py with py, perpendicular to the axis) gives a π orbital, whose density is not on the axis — there is a nodal plane containing the bond axis. An antibonding orbital always has one extra node cutting across the bond: σ* has one between the nuclei; π* has two nodal planes (one along the axis, one between the atoms).

Slide typos to ignore: slides 13–14 label the pz pictures “σpx”; slide 15’s two sub-bullets both say “bonding” (the second means antibonding); slide 18 labels the antibonding π pictures “π*npz” for both x and y.

The four MO rules of thumb (L12 slide 21)

  1. Conservation: the number of MOs equals the number of atomic orbitals you started with (two 1s → two MOs; six p orbitals → six MOs).
  2. “The higher the orbital, the larger the splitting.” Splitting = the energy gap between the bonding and antibonding partners.
  3. Symmetric split: bonding MO drops by the same amount the antibonding MO rises (a simplification, and it is how the diagrams are drawn).
  4. Best mixing happens between orbitals of similar energy.

Reading and filling an MO diagram

An MO energy diagram has the same three columns every time: left = one atom’s own orbitals, right = the other atom’s, middle = the molecule’s MOs. Energy goes up the page; each horizontal line is one orbital; dashed lines show which atomic orbitals combine; arrows are electrons. Then you fill it with the same three rules you used for atoms in Unit 02: lowest first (Aufbau), two per orbital with opposite spins (Pauli), and equal-energy orbitals get one electron each before any pair up (Hund).

For two atoms using only their p orbitals (L12 slide 19) the order from the bottom is σ < π (two, equal energy) < π* (two, equal energy) < σ*. Six p orbitals in, six MOs out.

Intermolecular Forces

L13

Unit 05 ended with a question the L10 slide asked and left hanging: “Why would molecules even aggregate?” A water molecule is neutral and has no ionic bonds to its neighbors — yet water forms a liquid and ice. The answer is a family of weak attractions between molecules. They are far weaker than the covalent bonds inside a molecule, but they decide whether a substance is a gas, a liquid, or a solid at room temperature.

Gap-filler · two words you need first

Dipole. A molecule (or bond) with one slightly positive end (δ+) and one slightly negative end (δ−), because the electrons are shared unevenly. Polar molecules like H₂O, NH₃, HCl are dipoles; symmetric ones like CO₂ and CH₄ are not (Unit 05).

Boiling point as a thermometer for sticking. To boil a liquid you must pull molecules apart from each other. So the stronger the forces between molecules, the higher the boiling point. Almost every question in this unit is secretly “which one boils higher, and why?”

Force 1 · Dipole–dipole

The L13 slide: “Molecules with permanent dipole moments will attract each other and create strong intermolecular interactions.” Each polar molecule is a tiny magnet with a plus end and a minus end. Plus ends attract minus ends of neighbors.

Bigger dipole = stronger attraction. That is why polar molecules usually boil higher than nonpolar ones of similar size.

Force 2 · The hydrogen bond

A hydrogen bond is an especially strong dipole–dipole attraction. It needs two ingredients:

  • A donor: a hydrogen attached to N, O or F (very electronegative, so that H is left strongly δ+).
  • An acceptor: a lone pair on a nearby N, O or F, which the δ+ hydrogen points at.
Cheat code · spotting a hydrogen bond

“H on F, O or N — and a lone pair on F, O or N next door.” Check both halves. CH₄ has hydrogens but no N/O/F, so no hydrogen bonding. Dimethyl ether CH₃–O–CH₃ has an O with lone pairs but its H’s sit on carbon, so it can only accept, never donate: not enough to bond to itself.

Why water is the champion

The lecture says: “Each water molecule acts as donor twice and as acceptor twice. NH₃ and HF each make two hydrogen bonds at a time on average.” Here is where those numbers come from, by counting:

The evidence: boiling points of hydrides

Compare the hydrogen compounds of the elements in groups 14–17, going down the periodic table. Bigger molecules should boil higher (we will see why in a moment), and group 14 does exactly that. The others do not.

Worked example — why does H₂O boil 160 °C above H₂S?

H₂S is a bigger molecule with more electrons, so on size alone it should boil higher than H₂O. It boils at about −60 °C against water’s 100 °C.

The reason: oxygen is small and very electronegative, so its H atoms are strongly δ+ and its lone pairs are accessible: hydrogen bonding. Sulfur is larger and less electronegative, so H₂S has only the weaker dipole–dipole and dispersion forces. The extra hydrogen-bond attractions must be broken before water can boil.

Force 3 · Dispersion (London) forces

Everything so far needed a permanent dipole. But helium, argon, and methane have none — and they still liquefy if you cool them enough. L13 slide 7 asked exactly that (“Why do noble gases or diatomic gases liquefy? What holds graphite together?”). The answer applies to every atom and molecule:

Polarizability

How easily an electron cloud can be distorted into a dipole. The lecture: “the amount of instantaneous dipole is proportional to polarizability.” Big, floppy electron clouds distort more (more electrons, further from the nucleus), so bigger atoms and longer molecules have stronger dispersion forces.

Two things make dispersion stronger

  1. More electrons / bigger cloud (“larger electron clouds will polarize more”). Methane (16 g/mol, boils −161.5 °C) → ethane (30, −88.6) → propane (44, −42.1) → butane (58, −0.5).
  2. Closer alignment / more contact area (“closer alignment will result in higher interaction”). Same mass, different shape:

What holds graphite together? Inside each layer, carbon atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds. Between layers there is only dispersion (the layers are stacked, as the slide’s drawing shows). extra That is why graphite flakes off in sheets and feels slippery, while diamond (covalent in all directions) is the hardest natural material.

Traps
  • Hydrogen bond ≠ “any bond containing hydrogen”. The H must be on N, O or F, and it is the attraction between molecules, not the covalent O–H inside one.
  • Boiling does not break covalent bonds. Boiling water leaves H₂O intact; only the hydrogen bonds between molecules are broken.
  • “Nonpolar” does not mean “no forces”. Dispersion acts between everything, and for big molecules it can outweigh a small dipole.
  • Size beats polarity only when the size gap is large. Compare like with like first.

Context · proteins fold by these forces

L13 closes with biology. A protein is a long chain that folds into a precise 3-D shape, and the folding is steered by exactly the interactions above: hydrogen bonds (holding helices and sheets), London dispersion (tucking oily side-chains together), electrostatic attraction between charged groups, and one covalent link, the disulfide bond (S–S).

When a protein misfolds, its sticky surfaces can make aggregates that kill brain cells; clumps of misfolded α-synuclein (“Lewy bodies”) are a hallmark of Parkinson’s disease. The student paper presented (Sant et al., Nature Communications 2025) found that a small early oligomer cut neuroblast cell viability to about 25%, while the mature fibril did not in the reported comparison: “the most damaging aggregate may not be the largest one.”

Metals & Energy Bands

L14

The third and last bond type: metallic bonding. Ionic bonds move electrons from one atom to another. Covalent bonds share them between two atoms. In a metal, the valence electrons are shared among all the atoms at once. The tool to understand that is one you already have — molecular orbitals — pushed from 2 atoms to 10²³.

Gap-filler · what “conducting electricity” needs

To carry current, electrons must be able to speed up a little when you apply a voltage. In energy terms that means each electron needs an empty energy level right next to the one it sits in, to step into. A material conducts if there are such empty neighbors; it does not if every nearby level is full and the next empty one is far away. Everything in this unit is about which of those two cases a solid is in.

From 2 atoms to 10²³: how a band forms

Unit 06 showed that n atomic orbitals make n molecular orbitals. Two atoms: a bonding and an antibonding level. Add more atoms in a line, each bringing one s orbital and one electron, and the same rule keeps working:

  • Bottom of the band: every orbital in phase, no nodes — the most bonding combination. Top of the band: signs alternate, a node between every pair of neighbors — the most antibonding. Between them the number of nodes steps up level by level.
  • Bandwidth = the energy spread from bottom to top. extra Atoms that overlap strongly (close neighbors, bigger orbitals) give a wider band.
  • Note what is not there: no two levels are exactly equal and no electron “belongs” to a pair of atoms. Each level is spread over the whole solid — which is exactly the idea of a sea of shared electrons.

Filling the band: why Na is a metal (and so is Mg)

Now count electrons, filling from the bottom like always. Sodium has one valence electron per atom (3s¹). Each level holds two electrons, so the 3s band has room for two per atom. One electron fills it exactly half. The top filled electrons have empty levels immediately above — so a small push moves them: a metal.

Magnesium has two valence electrons (3s²). Then the 3s band alone would be exactly full, and you would predict an insulator. But Mg is a metal. Why? Because the 3p band is not far above, and in a solid the 3s and 3p bands overlap in energy (L14 slide 4, “s and p-block metals”). The electrons just fill the bottom of one merged band, with empty levels right above.

Metal, semiconductor, insulator: it is all about the gap

Band gap, Eg

The energy between the top of the filled band and the bottom of the next empty band, in eV. To conduct, an electron must first be given at least Eg (by heat or by light). Small gap: a modest amount of heating or light does it. Large gap: practically none can.

Group 14: the whole range in one column

Why does the gap shrink going down? extra A common explanation: bigger atoms have larger, more spread-out valence orbitals that overlap more strongly with their neighbors, so the bands get wider and the filled and empty bands move toward each other until they meet. The lecture just states the trend; the size effect is the same story as the periodic trends in Unit 02.

Worked example — what light can cross the gap? extra

A photon of wavelength λ has energy E = 1240 eV·nm ÷ λ. It can lift an electron across the gap only if E ≥ Eg, so the longest useful wavelength is λ = 1240 ÷ Eg.

Si, Eg = 1.1 eV: λ ≤ 1240/1.1 ≈ 1130 nm (infrared) → it absorbs visible light and looks dark gray.

Diamond, Eg = 5.4 eV: λ ≤ 1240/5.4 ≈ 230 nm (ultraviolet) → visible light (400–700 nm) cannot excite it, so a pure diamond is transparent.

Bonding in the elements: metallic, covalent — or ionic only in compounds

Reminder from Unit 04

A pure element can never be ionic (no electronegativity difference), so an element is either metallic or covalent. Ionic bonding shows up when two different elements combine. The metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) sit on the dividing staircase and can behave as either, as in Unit 03.

The L14 “Future Five” talk lists the properties that made metals revolutionary 5,000 years ago: luster, conductivity, and malleability. extra All three come from the same picture: electrons in a band are free to move (conductivity), can absorb and re-emit visible light (luster), and are not tied to particular bonds, so planes of atoms can slide past each other without a like-charge collapse as in ionic crystals (malleability).

Conductivity and resistivity

Conductivity vs. resistivity extra
σ = 1 / ρ
σ = electrical conductivity (S/m); ρ = resistivity (Ω·m). Big conductivity = good conductor = small resistivity. The lecture plots one (conductivity, slide 9) and tabulates the other (resistivity, slide 10).

L14 slide 9 plots conductivity against atomic number: tall spikes at atomic numbers 29 (Cu), 47 (Ag), and 79 (Au) — about 6×10⁷ S/m for Cu and Ag and about 4.5×10⁷ for Au; aluminum (13) is also high; nonmetals sit near zero; and the lanthanides form a low plateau.

Worked example — do the two slides agree?

Copper: ρ = 1.67×10⁻⁸ Ω·m → σ = 1 / (1.67×10⁻⁸) ≈ 6.0×10⁷ S/m — right at the copper spike on the conductivity plot.

Aluminum: ρ = 2.69×10⁻⁸ → σ ≈ 3.7×10⁷ S/m — the “second” peak near atomic number 13.

Alloys are worse conductors than pure metals. Compare iron (9.71) with 96% Fe–4% Si (62), and nichrome (100) with any of its parent metals. extra Foreign atoms disturb the regular crystal that the electrons’ waves travel through, so electrons scatter more. That is why nichrome is used for heating wire, where you want resistance.

Work function

Work function, φ

L14 slide 11: “Energy to extract an electron from the solid.” In band terms: the energy needed to lift the least-bound electron from the top of the filled levels out to the free-electron level outside the metal. It is the solid’s version of an ionization energy.

The chart on the slide is colour-coded from about 2 eV to 6 eV: the alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) are the lowest (about 2–3 eV, easy to pull an electron off), and the late d-block metals around Pt, Ir, Ni, Re, Au are highest (about 5–6 eV).

Photoelectric energy balance extra
KE = hν − φ    λthreshold = hc / φ = 1240 eV·nm / φ
Light with a wavelength longer than λthreshold ejects no electrons, however bright.
Worked example — blue light on sodium extra values

Sodium’s work function is about 2.3 eV (a typical handbook value, consistent with the slide’s cyan color). Shine 450 nm blue light.

Ephoton = 1240/450 = 2.76 eV

KE = 2.76 − 2.3 = 0.46 eV → electrons escape.

Threshold: λ = 1240/2.3 ≈ 540 nm. Red light (700 nm, 1.77 eV) is below it: nothing comes out.

Context · the oldest problem in metallurgy

The L14 “Future Five” talk (Oliver Van Peteghem) starts at Tepe Hissar in present-day Iran, about 5,000 years ago, where people first smelted copper-bearing minerals: ore + heat + reduction → metal + waste. The hard part is always separating the metal from everything that came with it: the metal must form droplets, coalesce, move through thick molten slag and settle before it cools, so viscosity, surface tension, temperature and time all matter.

Today the question is climate. The old iron recipe is iron oxide + carbon → iron + CO₂, and steelmaking produces roughly 7–9% of global greenhouse-gas emissions. The challenge: use electricity to drive ore → metal with oxygen, not CO₂, as the byproduct. (This is the same idea as the carbon-free aluminum electrolysis cell in L07, which makes oxygen instead of CO₂.)

Cheat Codes: the whole course in eleven lines

read after the units, then use as your recall deck

Memorizing 14 lectures one fact at a time doesn’t work. What works: notice that almost everything in 3.091 so far is three ideas wearing different costumes, and each topic then has a one-line “code” that rebuilds the rest.

1 · Opposites attractCharges pull together or push apart: nuclear pull, shielding, lattice energy, lone-pair repulsion, bond polarity. Energy ∝ (charge × charge) ÷ distance.
2 · Energy comes in stepsElectrons only sit on allowed levels, and you fill them from the bottom: Bohr levels, shells, subshells, MO levels. Lowest first, two per orbital, singles before pairs.
3 · Waves add or cancelOrbitals are waves. In phase they build density between nuclei (bond); out of phase they leave a node (antibond). n orbitals in, n orbitals out.
Periodic trends
Pull ÷ (distance × shielding)

Across a period: more protons, same shell → tighter. Down a group: new shell → looser.

So ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity grow toward the top-right (electronegativity peaks at F); radius grows toward the bottom-left (Cs). Full or half-filled subshells hold on extra hard → the Be>B and N>O zig-zags.

Quantum numbers
Floor → room → seat → spin

n = floor (1, 2, 3…). l = room type, must be < n (0 s, 1 p, 2 d, 3 f). ml = seat, from −l to +l. ms = ±½.

Checking a set? Run the four rules in that order and stop at the first violation.

Filling orbitals (atoms and MOs)
Fill the bus

Lowest seats first · two per seat, opposite spins · everyone takes their own seat before anyone doubles up. The same three rules fill electron configurations and molecular-orbital diagrams.

Configuration shortcut: read the periodic table as a map — s block, p block, d block one row behind, f block two rows behind. Cr and Cu steal an s electron to get half/full d.

Bond type
One subtraction: Δχ

< 0.4 covalent · 0.4–1.8 polar covalent · > 1.8 ionic. It’s really a dial: % ionic = 100(1 − e−Δχ²/4).

Ionic → think charges and distance. Covalent → think electron counting (Lewis).

Lattice energy & Born–Haber
Charges first, then size

Compare ionic solids by multiplying the charges (2×2 beats 1×1) and only then comparing radii (smaller = stronger). Higher lattice energy → higher melting point, harder to dissolve.

Born–Haber = a staircase: up (costs energy) to vaporize, ionize, split X₂; down (pays) for electron affinity and, biggest of all, the lattice: ΔHf = V + IE + ½B + EA + UL.

Lewis → shape → polarity
The pipeline

Lewis structure → count electron groups on the center → find the row of the staircase → each lone pair snips one atom → name the shape → symmetric? then nonpolar → hybrid = groups (2 sp, 3 sp², 4 sp³, 5 sp³d, 6 sp³d²).

Names: line · triangle · tetrahedron · bipyramid · octahedron. Lone pairs: five → equator, six → opposite.

Angles
180 · 120 · 109.5 · 90/120 · 90

Those are the ideal angles for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 groups (and the hybrid is sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d² to match). Squeeze rule: lone pairs and double bonds are fat, so they shrink the angles next to them: 109.5° → 107° (NH₃) → 104.5° (H₂O).

Molecular orbitals
n in, n out. Down pays, up costs.

Bonding goes down, antibonding goes up by the same amount. Fill from the bottom. Bond order = (bonding − antibonding) ÷ 2. Unpaired electrons = paramagnetic (O₂: two in π*).

σ = head-on, π = sideways. Antibonding = one more node across the bond. Hybridization = mixing on one atom; MO = mixing across two.

Forces between molecules
Size, then polarity, then H-on-N/O/F

Boiling point = stickiness. Three forces: dispersion (everyone; grows with electrons and contact area), dipole–dipole (polar molecules), hydrogen bond (H on N/O/F pointing at a lone pair on N/O/F).

Compare like with like: ask “any H–N/O/F?” first, then “polar?”, then size and shape (long beats compact). Water gives 2 + receives 2, so it is the champion.

Metals & bands
n atoms in, n levels out → a band

Fill the band from the bottom. Empty levels right above the filled ones = metal. Filled band, small gap (Si 1.1 eV) = semiconductor; large gap (diamond 5.4 eV) = insulator.

Gap down group 14 shrinks C → Pb. Work function φ: KE = hν − φ. Conductivity σ = 1/ρ (Ag, Cu, Au best; alloys worse).

Any calculation
Let the units do the work

Write every number with its unit and chain conversion factors until only the wanted unit is left. Cube length factors for volumes (1 m³ = 10⁶ cm³). Photon chain: λ → ν = c/λ → E = hν → ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ → eV.

Forgot a formula? Its units usually tell you its shape (g/cm³ → mass ÷ volume).

When you’re stuck on an exam question

Ask three questions in order: (1) What is pulling or pushing on what? (charge, distance, shielding)  (2) Which energy level or orbital does this electron sit in, and is it filled bottom-up?  (3) Is this a place where waves add or cancel? One of the three nearly always unlocks the problem.

Practice Problem Archetypes

from all 6 recitations + 3 problem sets

These are the question types that repeat, nearly verbatim, across every recitation and practice set in your folder. If you can confidently do all of these with new numbers plugged in, you are ready. Click to expand each one.

Dimensional-analysis / unit-conversion chains Rec01

Build a chain of conversion factors so units cancel down to what you want. Common traps: cubing a linear factor for volume (1 m³ = 10⁶ cm³, not 10²); ppm/ppb means parts per million/billion by mass unless stated otherwise.

Example: soil with a 200 ppm lead limit, 600 m³ at 1.2 g/cm³ density → convert volume to mass (watch the cubing!), then apply the ppm fraction to get the mass limit in kg.

Weighted-average atomic mass from isotope abundance PP01

Multiply each isotope's exact mass by its fractional abundance, and sum. See the worked Cu example in Isotopes & atomic mass.

Photon energy chain: λ ↔ ν ↔ E ↔ eV everywhere

c = νλ, then E = hν. Divide by 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ to convert J → eV. This exact chain reappears in nearly every problem set in some form.

Bohr model for hydrogen-like ions (Z² scaling) Rec02, 03, PP02

For any single-electron ion (He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺…), En = −13.6 eV × Z²/n². Use it to find ionization energy, the n reached after absorbing a given photon, or the wavelength of a given transition. Remember the sign-convention trap: take |value| when solving for λ.

Validate a set of 4 quantum numbers Rec03, PP02

Check: n ≥ 1 (integer); 0 ≤ l ≤ n−1; −l ≤ ml ≤ +l; ms = ±1/2 exactly. See the worked example in Quantum numbers.

Write an electron configuration from periodic-table position Rec04, PP02

Read the element's block and position directly off the table — don't copy a memorized value. Remember the Cr/Cu/Mo/Ag/Au half-filled-or-full exception, and that ionized transition metals lose 4s electrons before 3d.

Hund's Rule orbital box diagrams (spot the error) Rec03

Degenerate orbitals fill singly (same spin) before any doubles up. A common "find the mistake" question shows p-orbitals paired up before all three are singly occupied.

Read/identify an element from a PES spectrum Rec04, PP02

Peak height/area gives electron count per orbital (reconstruct the configuration); total electrons ± the ion's charge gives Z. Isoelectronic species share the same pattern but shift position based on nuclear charge. See the worked example in PES.

Rank / explain a periodic trend PP02, Rec04

Always explain using the two-factor framework: nuclear charge (across a period) vs. shielding/distance (down a group). Watch for the Be>B and N>O ionization-energy exceptions.

Draw a Lewis dot structure for a bare atom or ion Rec05

Dots = valence electrons, one per side before pairing. Check that the resulting ion is isoelectronic with the nearest noble gas (except some transition metals, which instead favor half- or fully-filled d-subshells, e.g. Co²⁺).

Draw/label a bond-energy-vs-distance curve Rec05, Rec06

Identify the repulsive wall (short r), the minimum at r₀ (equilibrium bond length), and label the well depth as the bond energy. Same shape for ionic (Na⁺/Cl⁻) and covalent (H₂) bonds.

Solve a Born–Haber cycle for an unknown term Rec06, PP03

Usually solving for lattice energy, but could be ΔHf or an ionization/affinity term instead. Know both the simple 1-electron case (NaCl) and the harder 2-electron case (CaO, with two ionization energies and two electron-affinity steps). See the worked example in Born–Haber cycle.

Solve the Born–Landé equation for an unknown ionic radius PP03

Given lattice energy, M, and n, solve for r₀ = r⁺+r⁻, then subtract a known radius to isolate the unknown one. See the worked Cs⁺ example in Lattice energy.

Predict crystal structure from the radius-ratio rule reference table, L08

Compute r⁺/r⁻, then match against the table in Crystal structures to get the structure type and coordination number.

Draw a full Lewis structure for a molecule/ion (incl. resonance & expanded octets) Rec06, PP03

Run the full 6-step algorithm in Drawing Lewis structures. Watch for oxyanions (NO₃⁻, BrO₃⁻-type) needing multiple resonance structures, and period-3+ central atoms (P, S, I…) that may need an expanded octet.

Predict a molecule’s shape, angle, hybridization and polarity L11–L12 · no recitation yet

Pipeline: (1) Lewis structure (Unit 04). (2) Electron groups on the center = atoms attached + lone pairs. (3) Find that row of the staircase; each lone pair removes one atom; name the shape from the atoms only. (4) Ideal angle from the row, then nudge it down for lone pairs/double bonds. (5) Hybrid = groups (2 sp · 3 sp² · 4 sp³ · 5 sp³d · 6 sp³d²).

Worked examples: SF₄ and ICl₄⁻ in Practice: fifteen molecules. Common slips: forgetting the charge when counting valence electrons; naming the shape from the parent shape instead of the atoms.

Polar or nonpolar? Add the bond arrows L11

Draw the shape first. Arrows point at the more electronegative atom; add them as vectors. Symmetric with identical outer atoms (CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, PF₅, SF₆) → cancel → nonpolar. A lone pair on the center or different outer atoms (H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃, CH₂O, HCl) → polar. Polar bonds do not guarantee a polar molecule.

Fill an MO diagram: bond order and magnetism L12 · no recitation yet

Count valence electrons (O₂: 6 + 6 = 12). Fill the diagram bottom-up: two per orbital, one each in equal-energy orbitals first. Bond order = (bonding − antibonding) ÷ 2. Any unpaired electron → paramagnetic.

Check yourself: O₂ → (8 − 4)/2 = 2, two unpaired π* electrons. H₂ → 1. He₂ → 0 (no molecule). He₂⁺ → ½. See Reading and filling an MO diagram.

Ionic vs. covalent: predict melting point, solubility, conduction L10

First decide: ionic, network covalent, or molecular covalent? Ionic → high m.p., dissolves in polar solvents, conducts only molten/dissolved, brittle. Network (diamond, silica) → very high m.p., insoluble, hard. Molecular → low m.p./b.p., dissolves in like-polarity solvents, soft. See the comparison table.

Percent ionic character from Δχ L10

Look up both electronegativities, subtract, then 100 × [1 − exp(−Δχ²/4)]. HCl: Δχ = 0.96 → 21%. Si–O: Δχ ≈ 1.5 → 43%. Remember it never reaches 100%.

“Which theory explains it?” (Lewis / VSEPR / valence bond / MO) L12

Odd electron count (NO), unpaired electrons (O₂), half bonds (He₂⁺) → MO only. Octet exceptions (SF₆, BF₃) break Lewis. Geometry → VSEPR or hybridization. Bond order → MO. Table in Scoreboard.

Find the hybridization of an atom L12 · no recitation yet

Lewis structure → count σ neighbors + lone pairs on that atom → 2 sp · 3 sp² · 4 sp³ · 5 sp³d · 6 sp³d². Shortcut: 0 π bonds → sp³, 1 π → sp², 2 π → sp.

Practice and the full recipe: Hybridization, built from scratch (Part 5 and Part 8).

Name the intermolecular force / rank boiling points L13

Ask in order: ionic or network? → any H on N/O/F (and a lone pair to accept it)? → polar? → otherwise compare electrons and shape (long > compact). Water: donates 2, accepts 2.

Examples: HF > HCl; ethanol > dimethyl ether; n-pentane > neopentane; Ar > Ne. See Ranking and predicting.

Metal, semiconductor or insulator? Band picture and gap L14

Fill the band from the bottom. Empty levels immediately above the filled ones (or overlapping bands) → metal. Full band + small gap → semiconductor. Full band + large gap → insulator. Compare the gap with photon energy: λmax = 1240 / Eg (nm, eV).

Why Mg conducts although 3s² is “full”: 3s and 3p bands overlap. See Filling the band.

Work function and photoelectron kinetic energy L14, PP02 #8

KE = hν − φ. Convert the wavelength with E = 1240 eV·nm / λ. If hν < φ nothing is emitted. Threshold λ = 1240 / φ. See Work function.

Conductivity ↔ resistivity, and why alloys are worse L14

σ = 1/ρ. Cu: 1.67×10⁻⁸ Ω·m → 6.0×10⁷ S/m. Best: Ag, Cu, Au, then Al. Alloys (nichrome, Fe–Si, manganin) have much higher resistivity than the pure metals.

Consolidated Exam Traps

every "watch out" flagged by TAs, in one list
  • m³ → cm³: cube the linear conversion factor, don't apply it once.
  • Rydberg sign convention: absorption vs. emission flips the sign of ΔE; take |value| when solving for λ.
  • Keep units consistent within one formula — especially R∞/Rydberg constant in J vs. eV matching whatever form of h you're using.
  • Electron configuration must be derived from periodic-table position, not copied from a reference sheet — that's the actual skill being tested.
  • Ionizing a transition metal: always remove 4s electrons before 3d, regardless of the order they were filled in.
  • 1st IE of X⁺ = 2nd IE of X — a useful identity that shows up disguised in comparison questions.
  • Co²⁺ and other transition-metal ions don't necessarily follow "isoelectronic with the nearest noble gas" — they often favor half-filled or fully-filled d-subshells instead.
  • PES: peak height/area tells you electron count; peak position tells you binding energy/identity. Isoelectronic species share a pattern but differ in position.
  • Ionization-energy zig-zags: Be > B and N > O are real, expected exceptions — not errors — explained by extra-stable full/half-filled subshells.
  • ½ B(X₂) in Born–Haber cycles: remember the one-half factor whenever only half a mole of a diatomic gas (Cl₂, O₂, F₂…) is needed per formula unit.
  • O²⁻ formation is net endothermic for the 2nd electron-affinity step — counterintuitive, but still used as given data in Born–Haber problems.
  • Formal charge ≠ oxidation state. Formal charge splits bonding electrons 50/50; oxidation state assigns them entirely to the more electronegative atom.
  • Hydrogen is always terminal in a Lewis structure, never the central atom — it can only form one bond.
  • Expanded octets are only possible for period 3 and below (accessible d-orbitals) — never for C, N, O, or F.
  • A pure element cannot bond ionically with itself — ionic bonding requires an electronegativity difference between two different elements.
  • Count groups, name by atoms. Electron groups = atoms attached + lone pairs; the shape’s name uses only the atoms (4 groups with 1 lone pair = trigonal pyramidal, not tetrahedral).
  • “Bent” (and “linear”) appear twice in the VSEPR table — tell them apart by group count (bent: <120° for 3 groups, ≈104.5° for 4).
  • Double and triple bonds count as one electron group but are fatter, so they squeeze angles (H₂C=O: 116.5°).
  • Polar bonds ≠ polar molecule. CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, PF₅ and SF₆ have polar bonds that cancel. Draw the shape before adding arrows.
  • Lone pairs: five → equatorial, six → opposite. (Seesaw, T-shaped, linear AX₂E₃; square planar.)
  • The lecture’s loose angles: slide writes <180° for AX₂E and <120° for AX₃E; the tight values are <120° and <109.5°.
  • “Covalent” does not mean “low melting point”. Only molecular covalent substances melt low; network solids (diamond, silica) melt very high.
  • Hybridization makes no bond by itself — it prepares orbitals on one atom. MOs combine orbitals from two atoms. Number of hybrids (or MOs) = number of orbitals mixed.
  • Phase is not charge. Blue/red in orbital pictures = sign of the wave, not + / − charge; density = (wave)².
  • L12 slide typos: slides 13–14 label the pz pictures “σpx”; slide 15 says “bonding” twice (second = antibonding); slide 18 labels both antibonding π pictures “π*npz”.
  • Bond order = (bonding − antibonding)/2. Don’t forget to count the 2s-derived MOs (σ2s and σ*2s) — they’re filled in O₂ and cancel each other.
  • The σ2p / π2p order flips for N₂ and lighter (extra, not on L12 slides); the lecture only fills O₂.
  • Hybridization is a recipe, not a power: sp³ = one s + three p. Count groups (σ neighbors + lone pairs); double/triple bonds still count one neighbor each, because the extra bonds are π, built from leftover p orbitals.
  • The hybrid label gives the parent shape, not the exact angle. NH₃ and H₂O are sp³ but 107° and 104.5°, not 109.5°.
  • “Hydrogen bond” needs H on N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F next door. A C–H hydrogen does not count; neither does the covalent O–H inside one molecule.
  • Boiling and melting break forces between molecules, not the covalent bonds inside them.
  • Nonpolar molecules still attract (dispersion). Bigger and longer molecules attract more; compare size and shape before concluding.
  • A full s band does not make an insulator by itself: in Mg the 3s and 3p bands overlap. Look for empty levels right above the filled ones.
  • The band gap is a step between bands, not the width of a band. Metals have no gap at all; Eg shrinks down group 14 (C 5.4, Si 1.1, Ge 0.67, Sn 0.08, Pb 0 eV).
  • Small resistivity = good conductor. Do not mix up σ and ρ (σ = 1/ρ). Alloys conduct worse than their pure metals.
  • If hν < φ, no electrons are emitted, however intense the light.
