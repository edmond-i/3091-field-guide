The big picture: why "solid state chemistry"?

3.091 is not a generic intro-chemistry course — it's chemistry aimed specifically at understanding solid materials (metals, ceramics, crystals, semiconductors). The six-part arc the course follows is:

  1. Atoms & the Periodic Table — what's inside an atom, and why the periodic table has the shape it does.
  2. Bonding & Solids — how atoms stick together (ionic, covalent, metallic).
  3. Crystalline & Noncrystalline Solids — how bonded atoms pack into 3D structures.
  4. Chemical Kinetics & Thermodynamics — how fast and how favorably reactions happen.
  5. Solutions & Phase Diagrams — what happens when materials mix.
  6. Organic & Polymeric Materials — carbon-based materials and plastics.

01

Atoms & the Bohr Model

L01–L03 · Witt LN1

Before anything else, you need a working picture of what an atom actually is and where that picture came from. This unit builds it from first principles — no chemistry background assumed.

What's inside an atom

Every atom is built from three types of particle. Two of them — protons and neutrons — are clumped together in a tiny, dense center called the nucleus. The third — electrons — move around that nucleus in a cloud that's enormous by comparison.

Proton (p⁺)+1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C1.673×10⁻²⁷ kg (1.00728 amu)Nucleus

Electron (e⁻)−1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C9.11×10⁻³¹ kg — about 1/1800 of a protonSurrounding cloud
Sense of scale

An atom is about 10⁻¹⁰ m across; its nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁵ m across — the nucleus is roughly 1/100,000th the size of the whole atom. If the atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a grape in the middle of the field. Almost everything you'd call the "size" of an atom is empty space occupied by the electron cloud.

Three numbers describe any atom:

  • Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. This is what defines which element it is — change Z, change the element.
  • Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons (A = Z + N).
  • In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons. An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons, so its electron count no longer matches Z — a positive ion (cation) has lost electrons, a negative ion (anion) has gained them.

A history in five models

Nobody has ever "seen" an atom directly. Everything we know about its structure was inferred from experiments, and the model of the atom changed five times as better experiments came along. Knowing this sequence is itself testable, and it explains why we believe what we believe about atoms today.

Dalton (1803): atoms are solid, indestructible balls — the smallest unit of matter, like a billiard ball. All atoms of one element are identical; different elements combine in whole-number ratios.

Thomson (1904): discovered the electron. Since atoms are neutral overall, he proposed the "plum pudding" model — electrons are small negative specks embedded in a sphere of spread-out positive charge, like fruit in a pudding.

Rutherford (1911) — the gold foil experiment: this is the experiment that killed the plum pudding model, and it's worth understanding in detail because it's a classic example of reasoning from indirect evidence. Rutherford fired a beam of alpha particles (positively charged, heavy — essentially helium nuclei) at an extremely thin sheet of gold foil, with a detector screen wrapped around it.

  • If Thomson was right (charge spread out evenly), the alpha particles should sail straight through with at most tiny deflections — like a bowling ball thrown through a cloud of flour.
  • What actually happened: most particles did pass straight through — but a small fraction deflected at large angles, and a few bounced almost straight back.

Rutherford's own words capture how surprising this was: "It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15 inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." The only explanation: most of the atom really is empty space, but all of its positive charge (and nearly all of its mass) is concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus. A head-on alpha particle hits that concentrated charge and bounces back; most particles, aimed at the empty space around it, pass straight through.

Radiation used in this experiment

Alpha (α) particles are heavy and positively charged (stopped by a sheet of paper); beta (β) particles are electrons (stopped by ~0.5 cm of lead); gamma (γ) rays are uncharged high-energy light (pass through paper and several cm of lead). Penetrating power: α < β < γ.

Bohr (1913): fixed the obvious problem with Rutherford's model — classical physics says an orbiting (accelerating) electron should continuously radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus in a fraction of a second, which plainly doesn't happen. Bohr proposed that electrons can only occupy specific, fixed circular orbits, and don't radiate while sitting in one. This model is mathematically powerful (see the Bohr model section) but still treats electrons as little balls on fixed tracks.

Schrödinger (1926): replaced fixed orbits with probability clouds — regions of space where an electron is likely (but never certain) to be found. This is the model actually used today, and it's where quantum numbers and orbital shapes come from.

Also from Lecture 2: Millikan's oil-drop experiment

Millikan (1909) sprayed charged oil droplets between two charged plates and adjusted the voltage until a droplet hung perfectly suspended (electric force balancing gravity). Every droplet's measured charge turned out to be a whole-number multiple of one value — proving that electric charge is quantized (comes in discrete chunks) and pinning down the charge of a single electron, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C.

Mendeleev's prediction (1869) — why the periodic table is trusted

Mendeleev built the periodic table by arranging elements in order of mass and grouping by recurring properties — and famously left a gap where an undiscovered element should sit (under silicon), predicting its properties in advance as "eka-silicon." When germanium was discovered 16 years later, it matched almost exactly:

PropertyPredicted (eka-Si)Actual (Ge)

This is the strongest possible evidence that the periodic table reflects something real about atomic structure, not just a human filing system — and it's the same kind of reasoning (predicting something before confirming it) that found the planet Neptune by its gravitational pull on Uranus's orbit a few decades earlier.

Isotopes & atomic mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z, same number of protons) with a different number of neutrons (different A). Chemically they behave almost identically, because chemistry is driven by electrons — but their masses, and sometimes their nuclear stability, differ.

Isotope notation looks like ⁠ᴬ_Z X (mass number top-left, atomic number bottom-left). A shortcut: for an element with essentially one dominant isotope, A ≈ the atomic mass you'd read off a periodic table. This shortcut breaks for any element with multiple common isotopes — which brings up the single most common calculation in this topic:

Worked example — weighted-average atomic mass

The periodic table's "atomic mass" for an element is a weighted average across its naturally occurring isotopes, weighted by how abundant each one is. Copper is 69.09% ⁶³Cu (62.9298 amu) and 30.92% ⁶⁵Cu (64.9278 amu):

(62.9298 × 0.6909) + (64.9278 × 0.3092) = 63.55 amu — matches the periodic table value for Cu.

This weighted-average idea is also exactly how you'd convert between a volume/count of something and a mass using density and molar mass — the kind of multi-step unit-conversion chain ("dimensional analysis") that shows up constantly in this course. The rule of thumb that makes these chains manageable: write every quantity with its units, and chain multiplication factors so that units cancel until only the ones you want are left. If you ever forget a formula, you can often reverse-engineer it from units alone (density in g/cm³ tells you density = mass ÷ volume).

Trap: converting cm³, m³, etc.

A linear conversion factor (say, 1 m = 100 cm) must be cubed when converting volumes: 1 m³ = (100 cm)³ = 10⁶ cm³, not 100 cm³. This is the single most common silent error in unit-conversion problems.

Light & energy basics

Before you can understand the Bohr model, you need the vocabulary of light, because the Bohr model is really a theory about why atoms absorb and emit specific colors of light.

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Two numbers describe any single color of light, and they trade off against each other:

Speed of light
c = νλ
c = speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s, constant)  ·  ν (nu) = frequency (waves per second, Hz)  ·  λ (lambda) = wavelength (distance between wave peaks, m)

Since c is fixed, high frequency always means short wavelength and vice versa. The full electromagnetic spectrum, from longest to shortest wavelength: radio waves → microwaves → infrared → visible light (≈400–700 nm) → ultraviolet → X-rays → gamma rays. Inside the visible band, wavelength is what we perceive as color: violet/blue ≈ 400–500 nm, green/yellow ≈ 500–590 nm, orange/red ≈ 590–700 nm.

The piece that makes quantum mechanics "quantum": light doesn't arrive in a continuous stream — it arrives in discrete packets called photons, each carrying a fixed amount of energy set only by its frequency.

Photon energy (Planck–Einstein relation)
E = hν = hc / λ
E = energy of one photon (J)  ·  h = Planck's constant = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s  ·  higher frequency / shorter wavelength = more energetic photon

Energies at the atomic scale are inconveniently small in joules, so chemists use a friendlier unit, the electron-volt (eV) — the energy gained by one electron crossing 1 volt of potential:

Unit conversion you'll use constantly
1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J
Worked example — the "photon energy chain"

A green laser pointer emits λ = 532 nm light. Find its energy in eV.

ν = c/λ = (2.998×10⁸ m/s)/(532×10⁻⁹ m) = 5.64×10¹⁴ Hz

E = hν = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(5.64×10¹⁴) = 3.74×10⁻¹⁹ J

E = 3.74×10⁻¹⁹ J ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 2.33 eV

This λ → ν → E → eV chain is, by far, the most frequently recurring calculation across every recitation and problem set you have — memorize the two formulas above and you can always get through it.

The Bohr model, in detail

Bohr's goal: explain hydrogen (the simplest atom — one proton, one electron) using five postulates:

  1. Rutherford's nuclear picture is correct (dense positive nucleus, electron around it).
  2. Classical electromagnetism does not apply to the orbiting electron — it doesn't radiate energy continuously.
  3. Ordinary Newtonian mechanics (forces, circular motion) does apply to describe the orbit itself.
  4. Electrons occupy only certain fixed circular orbits, indexed by a whole number n = 1, 2, 3, … (the principal quantum number) — never anything in between.
  5. An electron only radiates or absorbs energy when it jumps between orbits, and the energy of the photon involved exactly equals the energy gap between the two orbits: ΔE = hν.

The mathematical trick behind Bohr's model: an orbiting electron needs a centripetal force pulling it inward (supplied here by the electrical attraction between the negative electron and positive nucleus — Coulomb's law). Combine that force-balance with the postulate that angular momentum is quantized,

Quantized angular momentum
mvr = n(h/2π), n = 1, 2, 3, …
m = electron mass  ·  v = electron speed  ·  r = orbit radius

...and solving the algebra (full derivation in Witt's lecture notes, not required to memorize, but shown here once so the result doesn't feel like it fell from the sky) gives two clean results for hydrogen:

Orbit radius
rn = n² × a₀, a₀ = 0.529 Å (the "Bohr radius")
Orbits get dramatically bigger with n — radius scales as n², not n.
Orbit energy — the single most important equation in this unit
En = −13.6 eV / n² = −2.180×10⁻¹⁸ J / n²
Negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus — you'd have to put energy in to remove it. n=1 (ground state) is the most negative, i.e. most tightly bound. As n → ∞, E → 0 (electron is free, no longer part of the atom).
nEnergy (kJ/mol)Energy (eV)Orbit radius

Notice the energy gaps shrink fast as n grows (1/n² scaling) — levels bunch up tightly near zero. Ionization energy (the energy to fully remove the electron, n=1 → n=∞) is just E∞ − E₁ = 0 − (−13.6 eV) = 13.6 eV, hydrogen's famous number.

This model generalizes directly to any hydrogen-like ion — any atom stripped down to a single electron, like He⁺ or Li²⁺ — by scaling with the square of the nuclear charge Z:

Hydrogen-like ions (one-electron species)
En = −13.6 eV × Z² / n²
Z = atomic number (nuclear charge). A more charged nucleus pulls the single electron in much harder — note it scales as Z², not Z.

Emission spectra — seeing the energy levels

Here's the payoff: when an electron falls from a higher orbit ni to a lower one nf, it emits a photon whose energy exactly equals the gap between those two levels. Because the levels are discrete, only specific photon energies (hence specific colors/wavelengths) come out — not a smooth rainbow. This is exactly what's observed when hydrogen gas is excited: a handful of sharp, bright lines, not a continuous spectrum.

Rydberg equation
1/λ = R · Z² · |1/nf² − 1/ni²|
R = Rydberg constant = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹  ·  same equation as ΔE above, just rearranged into 1/λ form.

Transitions are grouped into series by their common final level nf — memorize this table, it recurs often:

Trap: sign conventions in the Rydberg equation

Wavelength must come out positive. For emission (electron falls, ni > nf), using the formula as written gives a positive result directly. For absorption (electron rises, ni < nf), either swap which level you call ni/nf, or just take the absolute value at the end. Emission and absorption are the same transition, same |ΔE|, opposite direction.

Quantum numbers: an electron's full address

Bohr's model only needed one number (n) because it only had to describe hydrogen's circular orbits. The real, 3D quantum-mechanical picture needs four numbers to fully specify where an electron lives and how it's spinning. Think of it like a postal address that gets more specific at each step: country → city → street → house number.

SymbolNameWhat it describesAllowed values
nPrincipalShell / overall energy & size1, 2, 3, … (any positive integer)
lOrbital (azimuthal)Subshell / orbital shape0 to n−1
mlMagneticOrbital orientation in space−l to +l
msSpinElectron's intrinsic spin+1/2 or −1/2 only

The l value is always written as a letter rather than a number — this lettering (s, p, d, f) is used everywhere from here on, so memorize it cold:

l =LetterShape# of orbitals (2l+1)Max electrons (×2)

Worked example — the quantum-number validity check (very high-yield)

This is one of the most frequently repeated question types across your recitations. The rules: n must be a positive integer; 0 ≤ l ≤ n−1; −l ≤ ml ≤ +l; ms must be exactly +1/2 or −1/2.

  • [1, 0, 0, 0] — invalid (ms must be ±1/2, not 0)
  • [1, 1, 0, 1/2] — invalid (l must be < n; here l=1 is not allowed when n=1)
  • [2, 1, −1, −1/2] — valid
  • [2, 1, 2, −1/2] — invalid (ml must satisfy |ml| ≤ l; here l=1 so ml can only be −1, 0, +1)

Orbital shapes & nodes

An orbital is the actual 3D region of space where an electron has a high probability of being found — it is the physical, real-world meaning behind the quantum numbers above. A node is a region where that probability drops to exactly zero.

  • s orbitals are spheres. Higher-n s orbitals are bigger spheres with internal nested nodes (like an onion) — 1s has no interior node, 2s has one, 3s has two.
  • p orbitals are dumbbells — two lobes with a flat nodal plane of zero probability straight through the nucleus between them. There are three p orbitals per shell (px, py, pz), one pointing along each spatial axis.
  • d orbitals are mostly four-lobed "cloverleaf" shapes (one, dz², looks different — a dumbbell with a donut around its middle). There are five of them.
Degenerate orbitals

Orbitals that share the same energy are called degenerate. All three 2p orbitals are degenerate with each other; all five 3d orbitals are degenerate with each other. This matters directly for the filling rules in the next unit.

02

Building the Periodic Table: Multi-electron Atoms & Trends

L04–L06

Hydrogen has one electron, so there's nothing to argue about where it goes. Every other atom has multiple electrons competing for the lowest-energy orbitals — three simple rules settle the competition, and those rules are the actual reason the periodic table has the shape it does.

The three filling rules

1. Pauli Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in the same atom can share all four quantum numbers. Practically: each orbital (fixed n, l, ml) holds at most 2 electrons, and if it holds 2, they must have opposite spin (+1/2 and −1/2).

2. Aufbau ("building-up") Principle

Electrons fill the lowest-energy available orbitals first.

3. Hund's Rule

Within a set of degenerate orbitals (like the three 2p's), electrons fill each one singly, with parallel spin, before any orbital gets a second electron. (Think of it like passengers on a bus each taking their own double seat before anyone has to sit next to a stranger.)

Aufbau is easy to state but the actual energy order of orbitals is not simply 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s… — subshells from different shells interleave. The rule that predicts the correct order is the n+l rule:

The n+l rule

Fill orbitals in order of increasing (n + l). If two subshells tie on (n+l), the one with the smaller n fills first.

Worked example — why 4s fills before 3d

3d: n+l = 3+2 = 5     4s: n+l = 4+0 = 4

4s has the lower n+l, so it fills first — confirmed by real configurations: K = [Ar]4s¹, Ca = [Ar]4s², then Sc = [Ar]4s²3d¹. This single exception (4s before 3d, despite n=3 "feeling" like it should come first) is exactly why the d-block (transition metals) sits where it does on the table, two periods "behind."

Worked example — a three-way tie

3d: 3+2=5    4p: 4+1=5    5s: 5+0=5

All tie at n+l=5, so ascending n breaks the tie: 3d, then 4p, then 5s. The same logic, run out further, explains why the f-block (lanthanides) sits detached at the bottom of the table: 4f (n+l=7) ties with 5d, 6p, and 7s, but wins the tiebreak with the smallest n — so the lanthanide series fills 4f first.

The classic memorization aid for this order is the "diagonal rule" diagram — write subshells in rows by n (1s / 2s 2p / 3s 3p 3d / 4s 4p 4d 4f / …) and draw diagonal arrows through them top-to-bottom; reading off each arrow in sequence reproduces the n+l filling order automatically.

Writing electron configurations

An electron configuration is shorthand listing which subshells are occupied and by how many electrons, e.g. carbon = 1s²2s²2p² (2 in 1s, 2 in 2s, 2 in 2p — 6 total, matching carbon's atomic number). To save writing, you can replace the filled "core" with the symbol of the nearest smaller noble gas in brackets: Mg = 1s²2s²2p⁶3s² = [Ne]3s².

The skill the course actually wants

You are explicitly expected to derive a configuration from an element's position on the periodic table (which block it's in, how far across that block), not memorize or copy it off a reference table. Practice reading the table as a map of s/p/d/f blocks: Groups 1–2 fill s; Groups 13–18 fill p; the transition metals fill d (one period "behind"); the lanthanides/actinides fill f (two periods "behind").

Ions: for a cation, remove electrons from the atom's outermost shell first — note this is not always the orbital that was filled last. Transition metals are the classic trap: even though 4s filled before 3d going in, 4s electrons are always removed before 3d when forming a cation, because once 3d starts filling, it drops below 4s in energy — 4s becomes the "outermost," easiest-to-remove shell.

Exceptions to memorize: Cr, Cu, Mo, Ag, Au

A handful of elements break the naive n+l order because a half-filled or completely filled d-subshell is unusually stable (electrons spread across degenerate orbitals repel each other less).

Expected Cr: [Ar]4s²3d⁴  →  Actual Cr: [Ar]4s¹3d⁵ (both subshells half-filled)

Expected Cu: [Ar]4s²3d⁹  →  Actual Cu: [Ar]4s¹3d¹⁰ (3d fully filled)

Mo and Ag/Au follow the same pattern one row down (4d and 5d).

Measuring the unseen: ionization energy & PES

Nobody can directly "see" an electron's energy level. It has to be inferred by knocking the electron out and measuring how much energy that took.

Ionization energy

The energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom. The first ionization energy removes the outermost (easiest) electron; the second removes the next one from the resulting cation, and so on. Successive ionization energies always increase — you're always pulling an electron away from a more positively-charged (and therefore more attractive) ion than before.

Worked example — the big jump that reveals shell structure

Sodium's successive ionization energies (kJ/mol): I₁ = 495.8, I₂ = 4562.4.

Na is [Ne]3s¹ — only one valence electron. Removing it (I₁) is relatively easy. Removing the next electron (I₂) means reaching into the full, tightly-bound inner [Ne] shell — nearly a 9× jump. This single huge jump is direct experimental proof that electrons really are organized into separate shells, not just spread out uniformly.

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) is the main technique used to measure these energies directly, and reading a PES spectrum is a signature 3.091 skill.

The PES energy balance
Ebinding = Eincident photon − Eejected electron's kinetic energy
Shine a photon of known energy at a sample; measure the kinetic energy of the electron it knocks out; the difference is exactly how tightly that electron was bound.

A PES spectrum plots a peak for every distinct orbital in the atom — peak position (binding energy) tells you how tightly that electron is held; peak height/area tells you how many electrons are in that orbital (so a filled s-orbital's peak is twice the height of a half-filled p-orbital holding one electron).

Worked example — identifying an unknown from its spectrum

A PES spectrum for a 3+ ion shows four peaks (highest to lowest binding energy) in a 2 : 2 : 6 : 1 height ratio.

Reasoning: the heights give the configuration directly — 1s², 2s², 2p⁶, then a half-height peak = 3s¹. Total electrons = 2+2+6+1 = 11. The ion is 3+, so it has 3 more protons than electrons: Z = 14 → Silicon, as Si³⁺.

Trap to notice: neutral sodium (Z=11, 11 electrons: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹) has the exact same peak pattern/heights as Si³⁺ — both have 11 electrons in the same configuration (they're isoelectronic). The difference: Si has more protons pulling on those same 11 electrons, so every one of its peaks sits at a higher binding energy than sodium's. Pattern tells you the configuration; position tells you the identity.

The big four periodic trends

Every trend on the periodic table reduces to a tug-of-war between two effects, and once you internalize these two, you can re-derive (rather than memorize) every trend direction:

  • Nuclear charge (more protons = stronger pull): moving across a period (left to right), protons increase while electrons are added to the same shell — more pull on the same-distance electrons.
  • Shielding / distance (more inner shells = weaker pull): moving down a group, a whole new outer shell is added, farther from the nucleus and partly screened from it by all the inner-shell electrons.
TrendAcross a period →Down a group ↓Why
Atomic radiusdecreasesincreasesmore protons pull the same shell in tighter; a new shell makes the atom bigger
1st ionization energyincreasesdecreasesharder to pull an electron away from a more tightly-held, less-shielded atom
Electron affinity (magnitude)increasesdecreasessame logic as IE — greatest in the p-block, especially halogens
Electronegativityincreasesdecreasesstronger pull on shared (bonding) electrons

Ionic radius footnote: a cation (lost electrons) is always smaller than its neutral parent atom; an anion (gained electrons) is always larger — removing electrons leaves fewer electrons for the same (or greater, per-electron) nuclear pull to squeeze in, while adding electrons increases electron–electron repulsion and expands the cloud.

Trap: the ionization-energy "zig-zag" exceptions

Ionization energy doesn't increase perfectly smoothly across a period — it dips twice, and both dips are explained by extra stability of certain configurations:

  • Be > B: removing B's single 2p electron is easier than removing one of Be's 2s electrons, because it leaves behind a extra-stable, completely full 2s² behind, and the 2p electron is slightly higher energy/more shielded to begin with.
  • N > O: N's 2p³ is half-filled (extra stable, per Hund's rule reasoning); removing an electron from O's 2p⁴ actually relieves electron-electron repulsion (two electrons forced to pair up in one 2p orbital) and restores that stable half-filled state — so it's easier than you'd expect.

03

Ionic Bonding & Ionic Solids

L07–L08

Atoms almost never exist on their own in nature — they bond, because bonded states are usually lower in energy (more stable) than separate atoms. This unit covers the first of two bonding types: ionic, where electrons are fully transferred from one atom to another.

The octet rule

Atoms tend toward having a full outer shell — for most elements, 8 valence electrons (an "octet"), matching the electron count of the nearest noble gas. This is the single biggest driving force behind how atoms gain, lose, or share electrons.

  • Metals generally have few valence electrons and achieve a full shell by losing them, becoming cations.
  • Non-metals generally have many valence electrons and achieve a full shell by gaining a few, becoming anions.
  • Metalloids can go either way depending on what they're bonding with.

A Lewis dot structure is a simple drawing of an atom's symbol surrounded by dots representing its valence electrons — dots are placed one per side before any side gets a second (mirroring Hund's rule). For example: potassium (K) has 1 valence electron → one dot. K⁺ has lost it → no dots, with a + charge. Nitride (N³⁻) has gained 3 electrons to reach 8 → four pairs of dots, with a 3− charge.

Free radicals

A species with an incomplete octet — typically from an odd total electron count, which makes a full octet on every atom impossible. Free radicals are unusually reactive (they "want" one more electron to complete their octet) and show up in biology (reactive oxygen species) and battery chemistry.

Why ions actually bond: the energy story

Transferring an electron from one atom to another isn't automatically a good deal energetically — it only pays off once you add up all three steps involved. Walk through the formation of LiF, atom by atom:

  1. Ionize lithium: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻. This costs energy (ionization energy): +520 kJ/mol.
  2. Attach the electron to fluorine: F + e⁻ → F⁻. This releases energy (electron affinity): −328 kJ/mol.
  3. Net so far: +520 − 328 = +192 kJ/mol — actually unfavorable! Just forming the separate ions costs more energy than it releases.
  4. Let the oppositely-charged ions attract each other (Coulombic/electrostatic attraction, pulling Li⁺ and F⁻ together): releases a large amount of energy, roughly −700 kJ/mol.

Add it up: +520 − 328 − 700 ≈ −508 kJ/mol — strongly favorable overall. The electrostatic attraction between the finished ions is what actually pays for the whole process — not the electron transfer itself. This is the key conceptual point of ionic bonding: ionization energy and electron affinity are almost always a losing trade on their own; Coulombic attraction between the resulting ions is what makes the whole thing worthwhile.

Why ionic compounds form solid crystals, not isolated pairs

Once a Li⁺ and F⁻ pair up, that pair doesn't stop attracting other ions — a nearby F⁻ is still pulled toward the Li⁺, and a nearby Li⁺ toward the F⁻. The attraction keeps extending outward in 3 dimensions, which is exactly why ionic compounds form extended crystal lattices (like table salt grains) rather than individual two-atom molecules the way, say, H₂ does.

The Born–Haber cycle

The LiF arithmetic above is a simplified version of a more general and more exam-relevant tool: the Born–Haber cycle. It's just conservation of energy — the enthalpy (heat) of forming a solid ionic compound directly from its elements must equal the sum of every intermediate step's enthalpy, no matter what path you take to get there.

Born–Haber cycle (general form, 1-electron-transfer case, e.g. NaCl)
ΔHf = V + IE + ½B + EA + UL
ΔHf = enthalpy of formation of the solid  ·  V = enthalpy to vaporize/sublime the solid metal into a gas  ·  IE = ionization energy of the metal  ·  B = bond-dissociation energy of the diatomic nonmetal gas (halved, since only 1 atom is needed per formula unit)  ·  EA = electron affinity of the nonmetal (usually negative/exothermic)  ·  UL = lattice energy (always the big exothermic/negative closing step)
Worked example — solving for lattice energy (NaCl)

Given: EA(Cl) = −355, IE(Na) = +502, V(Na) = +107, ΔHf(NaCl) = −411, B(Cl₂) = +242 (all kJ/mol).

−411 = 107 + 502 + ½(242) + (−355) + UL

−411 = 107 + 502 + 121 − 355 + UL = 375 + UL

UL = −411 − 375 = −786 kJ/mol

This is the signature calculation of the unit — expect either this simple 1-electron version, or a harder 2-electron-transfer version (e.g. CaO, where you need both the 1st and 2nd ionization energies of Ca, and both electron affinity steps of O). One subtlety worth internalizing: adding a second electron onto an already-negative ion (O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻) is actually energetically unfavorable on its own (you're forcing a negative charge onto something already negative) — the overall compound still forms because the much bigger lattice-energy payoff more than makes up for it.

Lattice energy & the Born–Landé equation

Lattice energy can't be measured directly (you can't "undo" a crystal into gas-phase ions in a lab and watch a meter). The Born–Haber cycle above gets it indirectly, from other measurable quantities. There's also a direct, geometric way to calculate it, built from pure electrostatics:

Born–Landé equation
E = − (NA · M · Z⁺ Z⁻ · e²)/(4πε₀ r₀) × (1 − 1/n)
NA = Avogadro's number, 6.022×10²³  ·  M = Madelung constant (depends on crystal structure)  ·  Z⁺, Z⁻ = ion charges  ·  r₀ = equilibrium distance between ion centers  ·  n = Born exponent (short-range repulsion term, 5–12)

Two terms here need their own definitions:

Madelung constant (M)

A single ion in a real crystal isn't just attracted to its immediate neighbor — it's attracted to (or repelled by) every other ion in the entire lattice, near and far. The Madelung constant is the infinite sum of all of those pairwise interactions, collapsed into one number. It depends only on the geometry of the crystal structure (rock salt vs. CsCl vs. fluorite, etc.) — not on which specific ions are involved.

Born exponent (n)

Captures how sharply the repulsive force ramps up as ions get too close (electron clouds overlapping). It depends on which noble gas the ion's electron configuration resembles: He-like → n=5, Ne-like → n=7, Ar-like → n=9, Kr-like → n=10, Xe-like → n=12 (use the average of the two ions' values for a compound).

Worked example — solving the Born–Landé equation for an unknown radius

Find the ionic radius of Cs⁺, given: UL(CsCl) = 633 kJ/mol, M = 1.763, n = 10.7, r(Cl⁻) = 1.81 Å.

Rearrange for r₀, plug in (converting UL to J/mol and including NA):

r₀ = r(Cs⁺) + r(Cl⁻) = 3.50 Å

r(Cs⁺) = 3.50 − 1.81 = 1.69 Å

Because looking up the exact crystal structure (to get M precisely) is often impractical, a simplified shortcut called the Kapustinskii equation is sometimes used instead: fix n = 9 and approximate M ≈ 0.88 × (number of ions in the formula unit) — trading a little accuracy for not needing structural data at all.

Two trends fall straight out of this equation and are worth internalizing rather than memorizing as separate facts: lattice energy increases with higher ionic charge (Z⁺Z⁻ in the numerator) and increases as ions get smaller (r₀ in the denominator — closer ions attract more strongly). This is also why melting point tracks lattice energy so closely: both come from the same underlying Coulombic attraction holding the solid together.

Crystal structures

Once ions are set loose to pack together, geometry takes over: how many anions can physically fit snugly around one cation (and vice versa) depends mostly on the ratio of their radii. This single ratio predicts which of a handful of standard structures a compound will adopt.

Radius ratio r⁺/r⁻Structure typeCoordination #ExampleMadelung const.

Notice CaO and NaCl share the same rock-salt structure (coordination 6), but CaO's lattice energy is far larger than NaCl's — because the Born–Landé equation's Z⁺Z⁻ term is 4 for CaO (2×2) versus just 1 for NaCl (1×1), and that difference dwarfs the comparatively small difference in ionic radii. This is exactly why CaO melts at a vastly higher temperature than NaCl.

Properties of ionic solids

Every characteristic property of an ionic solid traces back to one fact: it's held together by strong, non-directional electrostatic attraction spread through the whole lattice.

  • High melting/boiling points — you have to overcome the full lattice energy to break the solid apart.
  • Soluble in polar solvents like water, which can surround and stabilize individual ions.
  • Poor electrical conductor as a solid (ions are locked in place, can't move to carry current) but a good conductor molten or dissolved (ions are now free to move).
  • Brittle — a shearing force shifts one plane of ions relative to another, lining up same-charge ions face to face; the sudden repulsion shatters the crystal cleanly along a flat plane, rather than deforming gradually like a metal.
  • Often transparent or vividly colored, depending on which ions are present and how they absorb light.

Mnemonic given in recitation: "CATions are PAWsitive" — a reminder that cations carry positive charge.

Covalent Bonding

L09

Ionic bonding is a full transfer of electrons. Covalent bonding is the other major strategy: two atoms share electrons instead, each gaining partial access to a filled outer shell without either one fully giving an electron away.

Electronegativity & classifying bond type

Electronegativity (χ, the Pauling scale) measures how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons in a bond — think of it as how hard an atom "tugs" in an electron tug-of-war. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (χ = 3.98); cesium and francium are among the least (χ ≈ 0.7–0.8).

The difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms, Δχ, predicts what kind of bond forms:

ΔχBond characterWhat's happening
< 0.4Pure (nonpolar) covalentelectrons shared almost perfectly evenly
0.4–1.8Polar covalentshared, but pulled noticeably toward the more electronegative atom
> 1.8Ionicpull is so lopsided it's effectively a full transfer

This also explains a fact worth internalizing: a pure element can never bond ionically with itself. Ionic bonding needs an electronegativity difference to create a cation/anion pair — two atoms of the same element have identical electronegativity (Δχ = 0), so the only options left are covalent (e.g. diamond, pure silicon) or metallic (e.g. iron, copper) bonding.

The bonding curve, with real numbers (H₂)

Covalent bonds follow the exact same shape of potential-energy-vs-distance curve introduced in Unit 3 for ionic bonds — only now the attraction comes from orbital overlap/electron sharing rather than Coulombic ion attraction. For H₂, the actual measured numbers are:

Interatomic distanceWhat's happening
r = 45 pmNuclei forced too close — strong repulsion, very high PE
r₀ = 74 pmEquilibrium bond length — PE minimum, = −432 kJ/mol
r = 300 pmEssentially two separate, non-interacting H atoms, PE ≈ 0

The well depth at r₀ (432 kJ/mol here) is called the bond dissociation energy — exactly how much energy it takes to pull the bonded atoms fully apart, and a direct measure of how strong the bond is.

What a covalent bond physically is

When two atomic orbitals (say, two separate hydrogen 1s clouds) get close enough to overlap, that overlap creates a new, lower-energy shared region — an electron-pair bond, with two electrons of opposite spin occupying the combined space between both nuclei. The energy drop from forming this shared, overlapping cloud is the bond energy.

Drawing Lewis structures: the full method

This is the single most practiced skill in the covalent-bonding unit. Follow these steps in order, every time:

  1. Arrange the atoms. Put the least electronegative atom in the center (with the exception that hydrogen is always terminal/on the outside, never central — H can only form one bond).
  2. Count total valence electrons. Add up each atom's valence-electron count; for a polyatomic ion, add one electron per unit of negative charge, or subtract one per unit of positive charge.
  3. Check for an odd total. An odd electron count means the octet rule cannot be satisfied everywhere — you have a free radical with an incomplete octet, by necessity.
  4. Draw the skeleton, connecting arranged atoms with single bonds (2 electrons each).
  5. Distribute remaining electrons as lone pairs, filling outer (terminal) atoms' octets first.
  6. Check the electron count against the central atom. Leftover electrons after every terminal octet is filled go on the central atom — even past 8, if needed (a hypervalent/expanded octet, only possible for period-3-and-below elements, which have accessible d-orbitals).
  7. If any atom is still short of an octet, convert a lone pair on a neighboring atom into an additional bond (a double or triple bond) to that atom, and recheck.
Worked example — HCN, built step by step

Valence electrons: H(1) + C(4) + N(5) = 10. H is terminal; C is less electronegative than N, so C is central: H–C–N skeleton.

A single-bond skeleton leaves C with an incomplete octet and bad formal charges. Moving lone pairs from N to form a triple bond between C and N fixes it:

H−C≡N: — formal charge 0 on every atom, full octets on C and N, H has its 1 bond. No resonance structures are possible here.

Quick sanity-check patterns

Nitrogen usually forms 3 bonds (1 lone pair); oxygen usually forms 2 bonds (2 lone pairs); halogens usually form 1 bond (3 lone pairs). If your structure doesn't match these defaults, double-check it with formal charges before trusting it.

Formal charge vs. oxidation state

These sound similar but use opposite electron-counting conventions — mixing them up is a common and easy mistake.

Formal charge
FC = (valence e−) − (lone-pair e−) − (bonding e− ÷ 2)
Convention: every bonding pair is split evenly, 50/50, between the two bonded atoms, regardless of electronegativity. A molecule's formal charges must sum to its overall charge.
Oxidation state

The opposite convention: every bonding pair is assigned entirely to the more electronegative atom (as if the bond were fully ionic, even when it isn't). This is why, for water, formal charge on O is 0, but oxidation state of O is −2 — same molecule, two different bookkeeping conventions answering two different questions.

When choosing between multiple valid Lewis structures, two tiebreaker rules apply: (1) prefer the structure with formal charges closest to zero (minimize both magnitude and the number of atoms carrying a nonzero formal charge), and (2) if a negative formal charge is unavoidable, it should sit on the more electronegative atom.

Resonance & hypervalency

Resonance

Some molecules/ions have more than one equally valid Lewis structure, differing only in where a double bond is placed (e.g. NO₃⁻ has three equivalent structures, each with the double bond on a different oxygen). The real structure is a blend — a resonance hybrid — of all of them simultaneously, not any single drawn version.

Hypervalency (expanded octet)

Elements in period 3 or below (P, S, Cl, and beyond) have accessible d-orbitals and can hold more than 8 valence electrons around the central atom — e.g. PF₅ (10 electrons around P) or SF₆ (12 around S). Period 2 elements (C, N, O, F) can never do this — they have no d-orbitals available.

A related, more advanced idea mentioned in recitation: aromaticity — rings (like benzene) with extreme, fully delocalized resonance, stabilized when they have 4n+2 π-electrons (Hückel's rule: 6, 10, 14…).

Write an electron configuration from periodic-table position Rec04, PP02

Degenerate orbitals fill singly (same spin) before any doubles up. A common "find the mistake" question shows p-orbitals paired up before all three are singly occupied.

Read/identify an element from a PES spectrum Rec04, PP02

Peak height/area gives electron count per orbital (reconstruct the configuration); total electrons ± the ion's charge gives Z. Isoelectronic species share the same pattern but shift position based on nuclear charge. See the worked example in PES.

Rank / explain a periodic trend PP02, Rec04

Always explain using the two-factor framework: nuclear charge (across a period) vs. shielding/distance (down a group). Watch for the Be>B and N>O ionization-energy exceptions.

Draw a Lewis dot structure for a bare atom or ion Rec05

Dots = valence electrons, one per side before pairing. Check that the resulting ion is isoelectronic with the nearest noble gas (except some transition metals, which instead favor half- or fully-filled d-subshells, e.g. Co²⁺).

Draw/label a bond-energy-vs-distance curve Rec05, Rec06

Identify the repulsive wall (short r), the minimum at r₀ (equilibrium bond length), and label the well depth as the bond energy. Same shape for ionic (Na⁺/Cl⁻) and covalent (H₂) bonds.

Consolidated Exam Traps

every "watch out" flagged by TAs, in one list
  • m³ → cm³: cube the linear conversion factor, don't apply it once.
  • Rydberg sign convention: absorption vs. emission flips the sign of ΔE; take |value| when solving for λ.
  • Keep units consistent within one formula — especially R∞/Rydberg constant in J vs. eV matching whatever form of h you're using.
  • Electron configuration must be derived from periodic-table position, not copied from a reference sheet — that's the actual skill being tested.
  • Ionizing a transition metal: always remove 4s electrons before 3d, regardless of the order they were filled in.
  • 1st IE of X⁺ = 2nd IE of X — a useful identity that shows up disguised in comparison questions.
  • Co²⁺ and other transition-metal ions don't necessarily follow "isoelectronic with the nearest noble gas" — they often favor half-filled or fully-filled d-subshells instead.
  • PES: peak height/area tells you electron count; peak position tells you binding energy/identity. Isoelectronic species share a pattern but differ in position.
  • Ionization-energy zig-zags: Be > B and N > O are real, expected exceptions — not errors — explained by extra-stable full/half-filled subshells.
  • ½ B(X₂) in Born–Haber cycles: remember the one-half factor whenever only half a mole of a diatomic gas (Cl₂, O₂, F₂…) is needed per formula unit.
  • O²⁻ formation is net endothermic for the 2nd electron-affinity step — counterintuitive, but still used as given data in Born–Haber problems.
  • Formal charge ≠ oxidation state. Formal charge splits bonding electrons 50/50; oxidation state assigns them entirely to the more electronegative atom.
  • Hydrogen is always terminal in a Lewis structure, never the central atom — it can only form one bond.
  • Expanded octets are only possible for period 3 and below (accessible d-orbitals) — never for C, N, O, or F.
  • A pure element cannot bond ionically with itself — ionic bonding requires an electronegativity difference between two different elements.
