How a 3.091 quiz works (and how to score)

  • 10 minutes, about 10 points, 4–5 parts. Most parts are worth 1–3 points. Lowest quiz is dropped, but you do not know which one yet.
  • Rubrics give points for steps: “identify the values”, “correct equation”, “answer”. A wrong final answer with the right steps still scores. Write the formula and the numbers, even if you are unsure.
  • About half the points are recall in one line: AXE number, shape, n- or p-type, strongest force, interstitial or substitutional, harder or softer. These are the cheapest points; drill them first.
  • “Briefly explain” parts need a reason, not only the answer (strained Si has a smaller gap because longer bonds are weaker).
  • Three habits fix most lost points: (1) units in every line, (2) degrees mode and θ vs 2θ, (3) answer exactly what was asked (shape vs electron geometry; charge vs subscript).

Moles, gas law & units

Quiz 1 · the recipe. Quiz 1 starts with a unit-juggling problem. Recipe: (1) write PV = nRT and solve for n; (2) check units (kPa·L = J, so R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)); (3) convert moles to grams with the molar mass; (4) a diatomic gas (H2, O2, N2) has TWO atoms per molecule. Read the full explanation →

Typical slips:

  • Using 1 g/mol for H2 / 16 for O2 instead of the diatomic molar mass.
  • Celsius in PV = nRT.
  • Linear conversion factor for a volume (cube it).
GAS-1●○○Quiz 1 style
An oxygen tank holds 20 L at 3000 kPa and 290 K. Assuming the ideal gas law, how many grams of O2 are in the tank? (Hint from the real quiz: kPa·L = J.)
g
Hint
n = PV/RT. What is the molar mass of O2, not O?
Worked solution
  1. Solve for moles: n = PV/(RT).
  2. Units: 3000 kPa × 20 L = 60000 J, which matches R in J/(mol·K). No conversion needed.
  3. n = 60000 / (8.314 × 290) = 24.89 mol.
  4. O2 has molar mass 2 × 16.00 = 32.00 g/mol. Mass = 24.89 × 32.00 = 796.3 g.

Answer: about 796 g.

Typical slips: using 16 g/mol (forgetting O2 is two atoms), using °C instead of kelvin, or using R = 0.0821 with kPa.

GAS-2●○○Quiz 1 style
Aluminum has density 2.70 g/cm³. What is the mass, in kg, of a block with volume 0.040 m³?
kg
Hint
1 m³ is NOT 100 cm³.
Worked solution
  1. Convert the volume: 1 m = 100 cm, so 1 m³ = 100³ = 106 cm³. (Cube the conversion factor!)
  2. 0.040 m³ = 0.040 × 106 = 4.0×104 cm³.
  3. Mass = 2.70 g/cm³ × 4.0×104 cm³ = 1.08×105 g = 108 kg.
GAS-3●○○Quiz 1 style
How many atoms are in 10.0 g of silicon (molar mass 28.09 g/mol)? Give your answer in scientific notation, e.g. 2.1e23.
atoms
Hint
grams → moles → atoms.
Worked solution
  1. Moles = mass / molar mass = 10.0 / 28.09 = 0.356 mol.
  2. Atoms = moles × NA = 0.356 × 6.022×1023 = 2.14×1023 atoms.
GAS-4●○○Rec 1 style
A 2.0 kg silicon wafer batch is doped with 5.0 mg of boron (by mass). What is the dopant concentration in ppm (parts per million, by mass)?
ppm
Hint
Convert both masses to grams first.
Worked solution

ppm = (mass of dopant / total mass) × 106. Put both in the same unit first: 5.0 mg = 5.0×10−3 g and 2.0 kg = 2000 g.

(5.0×10−3 / 2000) × 106 = 2.5 ppm

GAS-5●○○Quiz 1 style
In the ideal gas law with pressure in kPa and volume in L, which value of R can you use with no unit conversion?
Hint
Convert 1 kPa·L into SI base units.
Worked solution

1 kPa = 1000 Pa and 1 L = 10−3 m³, so 1 kPa·L = 1000 × 10−3 Pa·m³ = 1 J. That is exactly why the quiz gives the hint “J = kPa·L”.

Rule: before plugging in, make sure the units of R cancel the units of P, V and T.

GAS-6●●○Quiz 1 style
2.00 mol of an ideal gas is held in a 5.0 L container at 25 °C. What is the pressure in kPa?
kPa
Hint
Never put °C into PV = nRT.
Worked solution
  1. Temperature in kelvin: 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K.
  2. P = nRT/V = (2.00 × 8.314 × 298.15) / 5.0 L = 991.5 kPa (units: J/L = kPa).

Bohr model & photons

Quiz 1 · the recipe. Quiz 1 part 2: a hydrogen-like ion (one electron) jumps between levels. Recipe: (1) identify Z, ni, nf; (2) ΔE = −13.6 eV × Z² × (1/nf² − 1/ni²); (3) negative = emission, take the size for the photon; (4) λ = 1240 eV·nm / E; (5) visible light is roughly 1.65–3.1 eV (400–750 nm). Read the full explanation →

Typical slips:

  • Forgetting to square Z, or using the ion charge instead of Z.
  • Swapping ni and nf: the sign flips.
  • Thinking high energy = long wavelength.
BOHR-1●○○Quiz 1 style
Li2+ (Z = 3, only one electron left) drops from n = 3 to n = 2. How much energy does the emitted photon carry, in eV? (R = 13.6 eV.)
eV
Hint
Z² = 9. Compute 1/4 − 1/9 first.
Worked solution
  1. Identify: Z = 3, ni = 3, nf = 2.
  2. ΔE = −13.6 × 3² × (1/2² − 1/3²) = −13.6 × 9 × (0.25 − 0.1111) = −13.6 × 9 × 0.1389 = −17 eV.
  3. The minus sign means the ion loses energy, so a photon is emitted. The photon carries 17 eV.

Typical slips: forgetting to square Z (gives 1.89 eV), swapping ni and nf (flips the sign), or using Z = charge (+2) instead of the atomic number 3.

BOHR-2●●○Quiz 1 style
What is the wavelength in nm of the photon from the previous question (use hc = 1240 eV·nm)?
nm
Hint
High energy means SHORT wavelength.
Worked solution

λ = hc / E = 1240 eV·nm / 17 eV = 72.9 nm. That is extreme ultraviolet.

BOHR-3●○○Quiz 1 style
A transition emits a photon of 17 eV. Could you see it with an optical spectrometer (visible light is about 1.65–3.1 eV)?
Hint
Convert to nm: 1240 / E.
Worked solution

λ = 1240/17 ≈ 73 nm, well below the 400 nm violet end of the visible range, so it is ultraviolet. High energy ↔ short wavelength.

BOHR-4●●○Rec 2/3 style
In hydrogen, an electron falls from n = 4 to n = 2 (a Balmer line). What is the wavelength in nm of the emitted light?
nm
Worked solution
  1. Z = 1: E = 13.6 × (1/4 − 1/16) = 13.6 × 0.1875 = 2.55 eV.
  2. λ = 1240 / 2.55 = 486.3 nm, blue-green visible light.
BOHR-5●○○Rec 3 style
What is the ionization energy of He+ (Z = 2, one electron) from its ground state, in eV? (Ionization means n = 1 → n = ∞.)
eV
Worked solution

Z² = 4 and 1/nf² − 1/ni² with n = 1 → ∞ is 1 − 0 = 1 in size, so E = 13.6 × 4 = 54.4 eV. Compare hydrogen, 13.6 eV: the doubled nuclear charge holds the electron 4× tighter.

BOHR-6●○○Quiz 1 style
In hydrogen, which transition emits the highest-energy photon?
Hint
Compute 1/nf² − 1/ni² for each.
Worked solution

E ∝ (1/nf² − 1/ni²): 2→1 gives 0.75, 3→2 gives 0.139, 4→2 gives 0.188, 5→4 gives 0.0306. The biggest drop is into the lowest level.

BOHR-7●●○Rec 2 style
Green light has wavelength 500 nm. What is the photon energy in eV? (Also try: its frequency is ν = c/λ = 6×1014 Hz.)
eV
Worked solution

E = hc/λ = 1240 eV·nm / 500 nm = 2.48 eV. Check with frequency: E = hν = 4.136×10−15 eV·s × 6×1014 Hz = 2.48 eV.

Electron configurations & quantum numbers

Quiz 2 · the recipe. Quiz 2 asks you to (a) write a configuration, (b) draw the box diagram, (c) give the four quantum numbers for the boxed electrons, (d) pick a cation charge. Recipe: fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; spread electrons one per box before pairing (Hund); quantum numbers (n, l, ml, ms) with l = 0,1,2,3 for s,p,d,f, ml from −l to +l, ms = ±½; cations lose 4s before 3d. Read the full explanation →

Typical slips:

  • Putting 3d before 4s when removing electrons.
  • Pairing electrons before every 3d box has one (Hund).
  • Different ml for the two electrons in one orbital.
CONFIG-1●○○Quiz 2 style
What is the ground-state electron configuration of iron (Z = 26)?
Hint
26 − 18 = 8 electrons after the argon core.
Worked solution

[Ar] has 18 electrons, so iron needs 8 more: 4s fills first (2), then 6 go into 3d. [Ar] 3d6 4s2.

CONFIG-2●○○Quiz 2 style
How many unpaired electrons does a ground-state iron atom have? (Think of the five 3d boxes.)
unpaired
Worked solution

3d6: put one arrow in each of the 5 boxes first (Hund’s rule), then the 6th electron pairs with one of them. That leaves 5 − 1 = 4 boxes with a single arrow. (The 4s2 pair is full.)

CONFIG-3●●○Quiz 2 style
Which set of quantum numbers (n, l, ml, ms) is allowed?
Hint
Check each rule: l < n, |ml| ≤ l, ms = ±½.
Worked solution
  • (3, 2, −2, +½): l = 2 < n = 3 ✓, |ml| = 2 ≤ l ✓, spin ±½ ✓. Allowed (a 3d electron).
  • (2, 2, 0, +½): l must be less than n (max l = n − 1 = 1). ✗
  • (3, 1, 2, −½): ml must lie between −l and +l = −1..+1. ✗
  • (4, 3, 0, +1): spin is only ±½. ✗
CONFIG-4●●○Quiz 2 style
Which of these ions has a half-filled 3d subshell (3d5)?
Hint
Remove 4s electrons first, then 3d.
Worked solution

Cations lose 4s electrons first. Fe is [Ar]3d64s2: Fe2+ = [Ar]3d6; Fe3+ loses one more (from 3d) = [Ar]3d5. Co2+ is 3d7; Zn2+ is 3d10.

Watch out: the real Quiz 2 answer key justified Co2+ with “half-full 3d”, but Co2+ is 3d7. The +2 charge is right (lose both 4s electrons); the stated reason is not.

CONFIG-5●●○Quiz 2 style
For nickel (Z = 28): (a) write the configuration; (b) sketch the box diagram of 3d and 4s; (c) give the four quantum numbers for the unpaired 3d electrons; (d) is Ni more likely +1 or +2, and why?
Hint
Hund: one arrow per box first.
Worked solution
  1. [Ar] 3d8 4s2 (18 + 2 + 8 = 28).
  2. 4s: ↑↓. 3d (five boxes): ↑↓ ↑↓ ↑↓ ↑ ↑ (3 boxes paired, 2 boxes with one arrow, total 8).
  3. Two unpaired electrons, both n = 3, l = 2, different ml (two of −2..+2), both with the same spin ms = +½ (Hund). e.g. (3, 2, 1, +½) and (3, 2, 2, +½).
  4. +2: the two 4s electrons (the outermost) are lost first, giving [Ar]3d8. A +1 ion would have to keep a lone 4s electron, which is not a stable arrangement.

CONFIG-6●○○Quiz 2 style
When a transition metal such as Mn becomes a cation, which electrons are removed first?
Worked solution

Fill order and ionization order are different: 4s fills first but it is the outermost shell (n = 4), so it is the first to go. Mn2+ = [Ar]3d5.

CONFIG-7●●○Quiz 2 style
How many electrons in a ground-state chlorine atom (Z = 17) have l = 1?
electrons
Worked solution

Cl: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p5. l = 1 means p electrons: 2p has 6 and 3p has 5, total 11.

CONFIG-8●●○Quiz 2 style
Chromium is [Ar]3d54s1, not [Ar]3d44s2. What is the usual explanation?
Worked solution

Cr and Cu are the classic exceptions: half-filled (Cr, 3d54s1) or filled (Cu, 3d104s1) d subshells are especially stable. The exam asks you to derive configurations from the periodic table, so know these two as exceptions.

CONFIG-9●●○Quiz 2 style
Is Zn more likely to form a +1 or a +2 cation? Write the configuration of the ion you choose and give the reason in one sentence.
Worked solution

Zn = [Ar]3d104s2. +2: removing both 4s electrons leaves [Ar]3d10, a full, stable d subshell. A +1 ion would keep a lone 4s electron, which is easy to lose as well, so it is not stable.

Periodic trends & PES

Quiz 2 · the recipe. Quiz 2 part 2 gives three points for three arrows: electronegativity and ionization energy increase up and to the right; atomic radius is the opposite (increases down and to the left). Know the exceptions (Be > B, N > O), the isoelectronic rule (more protons = smaller), and how to read PES (position = binding energy, height = number of electrons). Read the full explanation →

Typical slips:

  • Reversing the radius direction.
  • Forgetting Be > B and N > O.
  • Comparing ions with different electron counts.
TRENDS-1●○○Quiz 2 style
Which atom is the largest: Na, Mg, Al or Si?
Worked solution

Same period, so the electrons are in the same shell but the nuclear charge grows from Na (+11) to Si (+14). More pull = smaller. Na is largest.

TRENDS-2●●○Quiz 2 style
Which has the higher first ionization energy, Be or B?
Worked solution

This is one of the exceptions to “IE increases left to right”. Be = 2s2; B = 2s22p1. The 2p electron sits higher in energy and is shielded by the 2s pair, so it comes off more easily. Be > B. (Same story for N > O.)

TRENDS-3●○○Quiz 2 style
Which element has the highest electronegativity: S, Se, Cl or Br?
Worked solution

Electronegativity rises to the right and up. Cl is right of S and above Br, so Cl is the winner.

TRENDS-4●●○Quiz 2 style
Which of these isoelectronic species (all 10 electrons) has the largest radius: O2−, F−, Na+, Mg2+?
Worked solution

Same number of electrons, so the one with the fewest protons pulls least: O2− (8 p) > F− (9) > Na+ (11) > Mg2+ (12).

TRENDS-5●●●Rec 4 style
A PES spectrum of a neutral atom shows five peaks with relative heights 2 : 2 : 6 : 2 : 1 (the 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s and 3p electrons). Which element is it?
Worked solution

Peak heights = number of electrons in each subshell: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p1 = 13 electrons = Al.

TRENDS-6●●○Quiz 2 style
Which has the higher first ionization energy, nitrogen or oxygen?
Worked solution

O is 2p4: one of its p electrons is paired, and the pair repels, so that electron is easier to remove. N (2p3, one electron in each box) is more stable: N > O.

TRENDS-7●○○Quiz 2 style
On a blank periodic table, draw the direction of the arrow(s) for (i) electronegativity, (ii) ionization energy, (iii) atomic radius, and give a one-sentence reason for each (the real quiz gives 1 point each).
Worked solution
  • Electronegativity: increases to the right and up. Reason: more protons across a period with the same shell; fewer shells up a group, so the pull is stronger.
  • Ionization energy: same directions as electronegativity (right and up). Reason: a tighter-held electron costs more energy to remove.
  • Atomic radius: the opposite: increases to the left and down. Reason: more shells down a group; weaker pull to the left.

You need two arrows per trend: one along a period (→/←) and one down a group (↑/↓).

After comparing with the solution:
TRENDS-8●●●Rec 4 style
The successive ionization energies of an element are (kJ/mol) 738, 1451, 7733, 10543. Which group is it in?
Worked solution

There is a huge jump (1451 → 7733) after the second electron: the third electron comes from a stable inner shell. Two easy electrons = group 2 (this is Mg).

Ionic bonding, Born–Haber & crystal structures

Quiz 3 · the recipe. Quiz 3 = Lewis dots + ion charges (K2O), reading a rock-salt picture (which colour is Cl−?), “which solid has the NaCl structure?” (check the charge ratio), and labelling a Born–Haber diagram. Recipe: group number → ion charge → balance the formula; Born–Haber steps = sublimation, ionization energy, ½ bond dissociation, electron affinity, lattice energy. Read the full explanation →

Typical slips:

  • Reading the subscript as the charge (K2O).
  • Mixing up ionization energy (metal) and electron affinity (nonmetal).
  • Forgetting the ½ for the diatomic.
IONIC-1●○○Quiz 3 style
What are the charges of the ions in Al2O3?
Worked solution

Group 13 loses 3 electrons (Al3+); group 16 gains 2 (O2−). Balance: 2 × (+3) + 3 × (−2) = 0. The subscripts are how many ions, not the charge (same trap as K2O: K is +1, not +2).

IONIC-2●○○Quiz 3 style
What is the formula of the compound formed from Mg2+ and N3−?
Worked solution

Find the least common multiple of the charges: 6. Three Mg2+ = +6 and two N3− = −6. Mg3N2 (cross the charges: the 2 of Mg goes to N, the 3 of N goes to Mg).

IONIC-3●●○Quiz 3 style
Which of these 1 : 1 ionic compounds does not have the rock-salt (NaCl) structure?
Worked solution

A 1 : 1 ratio is necessary but not sufficient. CsCl’s big Cs+ cation fits 8 Cl− neighbours (simple cubic lattice with a two-ion motif), while Na+, Mg2+, Ca2+ are small enough for 6 (octahedral). The real Quiz 3 answer (CaO, not CaF2) used the ratio test; CaF2 is 1 : 2 so it can never be rock salt.

IONIC-4●●○Quiz 3 style
In a Born–Haber cycle for NaCl, which step releases energy (is exothermic) on its own?
Worked solution

Taking an electron off an atom, vaporizing a solid and breaking a bond all cost energy. Adding an electron to Cl is favourable (energy released). The big payoff is the lattice-energy step (Na+(g) + Cl−(g) → NaCl(s)), which is also exothermic.

IONIC-5●●●Rec 6 style
Find the lattice energy of NaCl (energy released when gaseous ions form the solid, as a positive number in kJ/mol) from: ΔHf(NaCl) = −411; sublimation of Na = +107; ionization energy of Na = +496; ½×bond dissociation of Cl2 = +122; electron affinity of Cl = −349 (all kJ/mol).
kJ/mol
Hint
Add up the four steps before the lattice step: 107 + 496 + 122 − 349.
Worked solution

Hess’s law: the formation enthalpy equals the sum of all the steps.
ΔHf = ΔHsub + IE + ½BDE + EA + Ulattice

−411 = 107 + 496 + 122 − 349 + U → −411 = 376 + U → U = −787 kJ/mol

So 787 kJ/mol is released when the lattice forms. Remember the ½ for the diatomic: only half a mole of Cl2 is needed per NaCl.

IONIC-6●●●Rec 6 style
Lattice energy scales roughly as |q+q−| / r0. Estimate U(MgO)/U(NaCl) using r0(MgO) = 2.12 Å and r0(NaCl) = 2.82 Å.
×
Worked solution

MgO: charge product (+2)(−2) = 4, r0 = 2.12 Å. NaCl: product 1, r0 = 2.82 Å.

ratio ≈ (4 / 2.12) / (1 / 2.82) = 4 × 2.82 / 2.12 = 5.32

(The real values, about 3800 vs 790 kJ/mol, give ≈ 4.8; the simple proportion is close.) This is why MgO melts above 2800 °C and NaCl at 801 °C.

IONIC-7●●○Quiz 3 style
Which compound should have the largest lattice energy (strongest ionic bonding)?
Worked solution

Born–Landé: bigger charges and smaller ions → larger lattice energy. MgO has 2+/2− (charge product 4) and small ions.

IONIC-8●●●Quiz 3 style
Quiz 3 asks you to label missing steps on a Born–Haber diagram. List, in order, the steps that build K2O(s) from 2 K(s) and ½ O2(g), with the energy sign of each.
Worked solution
  1. 2 K(s) → 2 K(g): sublimation (+).
  2. 2 K(g) → 2 K+(g) + 2 e−: ionization energy × 2 (+).
  3. ½ O2(g) → O(g): ½ bond dissociation (+).
  4. O(g) + e− → O−(g): 1st electron affinity (−).
  5. O−(g) + e− → O2−(g): 2nd electron affinity (+, because you push an electron onto an already-negative ion).
  6. 2 K+(g) + O2−(g) → K2O(s): lattice energy (large −).

The sum of all six equals ΔHf(K2O). Typical mistakes: mixing up ionization energy (metal) with electron affinity (nonmetal), and forgetting the atomization step of the nonmetal.

Lewis structures, dipoles & bond strength

Quiz 3–4 · the recipe. Recipe for any Lewis structure: (1) total valence electrons (add for − charge, subtract for +); (2) skeleton with the least electronegative atom in the middle (H is never central); (3) single bonds, then fill octets outside; (4) leftovers go on the centre (expanded octet only for period 3+); (5) compute formal charges. Dipole: μ = δ·r with 1 D = 0.2082 e·Å. Stronger bond = smaller atoms, higher bond order. Read the full explanation →

Typical slips:

  • Missing the extra electrons of a negative charge.
  • Expanding the octet of C, N, O or F.
  • Calling a bond “ionic” when it is polar covalent.
LEWIS-1●○○Rec 7 style
How many valence electrons must appear in the Lewis structure of the sulfate ion, SO42−?
electrons
Worked solution

S: 6. Four O: 4 × 6 = 24. The 2− charge adds 2 electrons: 6 + 24 + 2 = 32.

LEWIS-2●●○Rec 7 style
In the Lewis structure [ O–C≡N ]− (single bond O–C with three lone pairs on O; triple bond C≡N with one lone pair on N), what is the formal charge on oxygen?
Worked solution

Formal charge = valence electrons − (lone-pair electrons + ½ bonding electrons) = 6 − (6 + ½×2) = 6 − 7 = −1. (N: 5 − (2 + 3) = 0; C: 4 − 4 = 0; the charges add up to the ion’s −1.)

LEWIS-3●●○Rec 7 style
The H–F bond length is 0.917 Å and its dipole moment is 1.82 D. What partial charge δ (in units of e) sits on the atoms? (1 D = 0.2082 e·Å.)
e
Worked solution

μ = δ·r so δ = μ / r = (1.82 × 0.2082 e·Å) / 0.917 Å = 0.413 e. About 41% of a full electron charge has shifted towards F (a polar covalent bond, not fully ionic).

LEWIS-4●●○Rec 7 style
Use % ionic character = 1 − exp(−¼(Δχ)²) × 100 to find the percent ionic character of H–F (χH = 2.20, χF = 3.98).
%
Worked solution

Δχ = 3.98 − 2.20 = 1.78. (Δχ)² = 3.17; ¼ of that = 0.792; exp(−0.792) = 0.453; 1 − 0.453 = 0.547. ≈ 54.7 % ionic. The bond is polar covalent, nearer the ionic end than Cl–Cl but not fully ionic.

LEWIS-5●○○Rec 7 style
Which carbon–carbon bond is the strongest (highest bond energy)?
Worked solution

Higher bond order = stronger (and shorter) bond: triple > double > single. Rec 7 ranks C2H6 < C2H4 < C2H2.

LEWIS-6●○○Rec 7 style
Which hydrogen halide has the strongest H–X bond?
Worked solution

Smaller atom → better orbital overlap → stronger bond. Ranking: HI < HBr < HCl < HF.

LEWIS-7●●○Rec 7 style
Which of these molecules needs an expanded octet on its central atom?
Worked solution

SF4: S has 4 bonds + 1 lone pair = 10 electrons, allowed because S is in period 3 (it can use d orbitals). BF3 is electron-deficient (6), CF4 and NF3 obey the octet. Period 2 atoms (C, N, O, F) can never exceed 8.

LEWIS-8●●●Rec 7 style
Draw the Lewis structure of the nitrate ion, NO3−. How many resonance forms are there and what are the formal charges?
Worked solution
  1. Valence electrons: 5 + 3 × 6 + 1 = 24.
  2. N in the centre, three O around it, three N–O single bonds (6 e−), then octets on each O (18 e−) = 24 total, but N has only 6.
  3. Make one lone pair on an O into an N=O double bond: N now has 8.
  4. Formal charges: N = +1, the double-bonded O = 0, the two single-bonded O = −1 each (total −1).
  5. The double bond can be on any of the three O atoms: 3 equivalent resonance forms; the real ion is a blend (all N–O bonds equal, bond order 1⅓).

LEWIS-9●●○Rec 7 style
Why are ionic solids typically more brittle than covalent solids?
Worked solution

Rec 7 says exactly this: a one-lattice-spacing shift puts + next to + and − next to −, so repulsion cascades into fracture (chalk is an example). Covalent bonds are directional and tolerate a few broken bonds. These are relative trends: quartz and graphite are brittle covalent solids.

  • Forgetting the ½ in bond order.

HYBMO-1●○○Rec 9 style
What is the hybridization of the central atom in NH3?
Worked solution

3 N–H bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 groups → sp3 (tetrahedral arrangement, trigonal pyramidal shape).

HYBMO-2●●○Rec 9 style
What is the hybridization of Se in SeF4?
Worked solution

4 bonds + 1 lone pair = 5 groups → sp3d (see-saw shape).

HYBMO-3●●○Rec 9 style
In ethyne (acetylene, HC≡CH), what is the hybridization of each carbon and how many σ and π bonds does the C≡C triple bond contain?
Worked solution

Each C has 2 groups (one H, one C) → sp. The triple bond is one σ (head-on overlap of sp hybrids) plus two π bonds (side-on overlap of the two leftover p orbitals).

HYBMO-4●●●Rec 9 style
What is the bond order of O2+? (O2 has 12 valence electrons; fill σ2s, σ*2s, σ2p, π2p, π*2p in that order.)
Worked solution

O2+ has 11 valence electrons: σ2s2 σ*2s2 σ2p2 π2p4 π*2p1. Bonding = 2 + 2 + 4 = 8; antibonding = 2 + 1 = 3. Bond order = ½(8 − 3) = 2.5. Removing an antibonding electron from O2 (BO 2) makes the bond stronger and shorter.

HYBMO-5●●○Rec 9 style
What is the bond order of He2+ (3 electrons)?
Worked solution

He2+: σ1s2 σ*1s1. Bond order = ½(2 − 1) = 0.5. A weak bond exists. (Neutral He2 would have BO = ½(2 − 2) = 0: no bond, which is why helium is a monatomic gas.)

HYBMO-6●●○Rec 9 style
O2 has two unpaired electrons. Where are they in the MO diagram?
Worked solution

The last two electrons of O2 go into the two degenerate π* orbitals, one each, with the same spin (Hund’s rule). That makes O2 paramagnetic, which the Lewis structure O=O cannot explain.

HYBMO-7●●●Rec 9 style
For which molecule is the π2p level drawn below the σ2p level (the “flipped” order)?
Worked solution

For the lighter diatomics (B2, C2, N2) the s and p orbitals mix, pushing σ2p above π2p. For O2 and F2 the normal order (σ2p below π2p) is used. Rec 9 says: “you don’t need to know why”, only that N2 flips.

HYBMO-8●●○Quiz 4 style
Draw how the 1s orbitals of two hydrogen atoms form the MOs of H2. Label everything, fill in the electrons, and give the bond order (this is 4 points on Quiz 4).
Worked solution
  1. Energy axis. Two H 1s levels, one on the left and one on the right at the same height, each with one electron (↑).
  2. Between them: σ1s (bonding) below and σ*1s (antibonding) above. Dashed lines connect each 1s to both MOs. The antibonding one is higher by the same amount the bonding one is lower.
  3. Both electrons go into σ1s with opposite spins (↑↓). σ*1s stays empty.
  4. Bond order = ½(2 − 0) = 1: a single bond.

Common mistakes: antibonding below bonding; two electrons with the same spin; forgetting the energy axis or the orbital labels.

Quiz 4 · the recipe. Quiz 4 problem 3: “strongest intermolecular force for each of these molecules?” (1 point each). Decision path: (1) ions? ion–ion / ion–dipole. (2) H on N, O or F plus a lone pair on N/O/F nearby? hydrogen bonding. (3) polar shape (draw it!)? dipole–dipole. (4) otherwise dispersion (bigger = stronger). Typical slips:

  • Answering “covalent bond” (that is inside the molecule).
  • Answering dipole–dipole when an N–H / O–H / H–F is present.
  • Calling a symmetric molecule (CF4) polar.
IMF-1●○○Quiz 4 style
What is the strongest intermolecular force in liquid methanol, CH3OH?
Worked solution

Methanol has an O–H bond: H bonded to O, with lone pairs on O of the neighbouring molecule. Hydrogen bonding is the strongest. (It also has dipole–dipole and dispersion, but the question asks for the strongest.)

IMF-2●○○Quiz 4 style
What is the strongest intermolecular force in SO2?
Worked solution

SO2 is bent, so its S–O bond dipoles do not cancel: polar. It has no H, so no hydrogen bonding. Strongest = dipole–dipole.

IMF-3●○○Quiz 4 style
What is the strongest intermolecular force in O2 (or N2, or CF4)?
Worked solution

O2 is a nonpolar homonuclear molecule. CF4 has polar bonds but a symmetric tetrahedral shape, so it is nonpolar too. Only temporary (instantaneous) dipoles remain. Covalent bonding is not an intermolecular force: it is inside the molecule.

IMF-4●●○Rec 9.5 style
Which has the highest boiling point?
Worked solution

Boiling point rises with the strength of the attraction that must be overcome: Ne (weak dispersion) < CH4 (dispersion) < HF (hydrogen bonds) < NaF (ion–ion, much stronger). The same idea ranks Ne < O2 < N(CH3)3 < NaCl < CaO in Rec 9.5 (CaO’s 2+/2− charges beat NaCl’s 1+/1−).

IMF-5●●○Rec 9.5 style
What is the strongest force between an HBr molecule and a CH4 molecule?
Worked solution

HBr is polar; CH4 is nonpolar. HBr’s field distorts CH4’s electron cloud and induces a dipole. Polar + nonpolar = dipole–induced dipole.

IMF-6●●○Rec 9.5 style
Among HF, HCl, HBr and HI, HF has the highest boiling point even though it is the smallest. Why?
Worked solution

Boiling breaks forces between molecules, not the H–F bond. HF’s H bonds win. Among HCl, HBr, HI the boiling point rises with size because dispersion forces grow.

IMF-7●●○Rec 9.5 style
Which of these molecules can form hydrogen bonds with itself?
Worked solution

You need an H bonded to N, O or F in the molecule. CH3NH2 has N–H. CH3OCH3 has O but no O–H; CH3F has F but the H atoms are on carbon (C–H does not count).

IMF-8●●○Rec 9.5 style
Explain why methane (CH4) is a gas at room temperature but C24H50 is a waxy solid, even though both are nonpolar hydrocarbons.
Worked solution

Both only have dispersion forces, but dispersion grows with the number of electrons and the contact surface (polarizability scales with molecular volume). A long C24 chain has far more electrons and a much larger contact area, so each molecule is attracted far more strongly to its neighbours; it takes more thermal energy to separate them, so it is solid. CH4 is tiny: only weak attractions, so thermal motion wins even at low temperature.

BANDS-1●○○Quiz 5 style
A band diagram shows a filled band that overlaps an empty band. Which of gold, germanium and diamond is this?
Worked solution

Overlap means there is no gap at all: electrons can step into empty levels with no energy cost: a metal. Germanium (0.67 eV) and diamond (5.4 eV) both have a filled valence band, an empty conduction band and a gap (small vs large).

Worked solution

BANDS-3●○○Rec 10 style

nm
Worked solution

BANDS-4●○○Quiz 5 style
Which dopant would make germanium n-type?
Worked solution

Ge is in group 14 (4 valence electrons). A group 15 atom brings 5: one extra electron, loosely held (a donor level just below the conduction band) → n-type. Groups 13 and 12 are short of electrons → p-type (acceptors). A dopant can also supply several carriers: Te (6 e−) is n-type, Zn (2 e−) is p-type.

BANDS-6●●○Quiz 5 style
Silicon is squeezed so the bond distance shrinks slightly (compressive strain). The band gap should…

BANDS-7●●○Rec 9.5 style
Be is 2s2, so a lone 2s band would be completely full. Why is Be still a metal?
Worked solution

A full band would mean bond order 0 (like He2) and no empty levels to move into. The overlapping 2p band solves both problems: some electrons occupy low-lying bonding levels of the p band, and plenty of empty levels sit right above.

BANDS-8●●○Rec 10 style
Heating a pure semiconductor generally makes its conductivity…
Worked solution

Temperature sets how often electrons get across the gap. Each event creates a free electron and a hole; both conduct. (Metals behave the other way round: more thermal vibration scatters the electrons, so conductivity usually drops. extra)

In cubic crystals only: the plane (hkl) is perpendicular to the direction [hkl]. Check with the dot product: the vectors lying in (111), such as (−1, 1, 0), have zero dot product with (1, 1, 1).

MILLER-10●○○L17 slide 12
How many faces does the {001} family describe for a cube?
Worked solution

{001} = (001), (001), (100), (100), (010), (010): all six faces of the cube. Curly braces = every symmetry-equivalent plane.

MILLER-11●●○L17 slide 14
Compared with d111, the spacing d222 in the same cubic crystal is…
Worked solution

d222 = a/√12 and d111 = a/√3, so the ratio is √3/√12 = ½. (Doubling all the indices halves the spacing: the planes of (222) include the (111) planes plus a plane midway between each pair.)

MILLER-12●●○L17 slide 11
A plane cuts x at 1 and y at 1 and is parallel to z. What are its Miller indices?
Worked solution

Intercepts 1, 1, ∞ → reciprocals 1, 1, 0: (110). Parallel to an axis means intercept ∞ and index 0.

DEFECTS-1●○○Quiz 6 style
Carbon (radius about 0.77 Å) is added to iron (radius about 1.24 Å) to make steel. Does the carbon form a substitutional or an interstitial defect?
Worked solution

C is much smaller than Fe, so it sits in the holes between iron atoms (carbon steel = interstitial alloy). Substitutional alloys need atoms of similar size, like Zn in Cu (brass).

DEFECTS-2●○○L18 slide 9
Brass is made by replacing some Cu atoms in the lattice with Zn atoms. This is a…
Worked solution

The foreign atom sits on a lattice site where a host atom would be: substitutional. Same idea as P in Si (a dopant is a substitutional point defect).

DEFECTS-3●●○L18 slide 8
Which statement is correct about Schottky and Frenkel defects in an ionic crystal?
Worked solution

Both keep the crystal electrically neutral. Schottky: two vacancies (+ and −), the ions leave. Frenkel: a vacancy + an interstitial of the same ion (usually the small cation).

DEFECTS-4●○○Quiz 6 style
A block of low-carbon steel is hammered extensively into a new shape. Compared with before, it is…
Worked solution

Plastic deformation multiplies dislocations; tangled dislocations cannot move easily, so further deformation needs more stress: work (strain) hardening. Annealing (heating) is what softens a metal again. L18 slide 16: “Cold worked Ti” shows the tangles.

DEFECTS-5●●○L18 slide 15
Why can a metal deform plastically at stresses 1000–10 000 times lower than the theoretical strength of a perfect crystal?
Worked solution

Slide 15’s cartoon: dragging a whole rug (all bonds at once) vs. pushing a wrinkle along it. A dislocation is the wrinkle: moving it only requires breaking and re-making bonds along one row.

DEFECTS-6●●○L19 slide 9
What happens when Na2O and CaO are added to silica (SiO2)?
Worked solution

Na+ and Ca2+ are network modifiers: each supplies an O that bonds to only one Si (non-bridging oxygen). Fewer links between tetrahedra → the glass softens at a much lower temperature (soda-lime is the leftmost curve on the viscosity plot) and is easy to work.

DEFECTS-7●○○L19 slide 4
Quartz and silica glass are both SiO2. What is the difference?
Worked solution

Same building block and same local bonding (Si–O 162 pm, O–Si–O 109.5°); crystal = periodic, glass = random ring sizes, no long-range order (but the local tetrahedron is preserved). Amorphous solids form when the structure packs badly or is cooled quickly.

DEFECTS-8●○○L19 slide 12–13
Why can an ordinary (non-crosslinked) plastic bottle be melted and recycled but a vulcanized rubber tyre cannot?
Worked solution

Vulcanization links the chains with sulfur bridges into a 3D network. You cannot melt a network without breaking covalent bonds. Chains that are only entangled (HDPE, PET) slide past each other when heated.

DEFECTS-9●●○L19 slide 10
On the viscosity vs temperature plot, which glass reaches the working viscosity at the lowest temperature?
Worked solution

Soda-lime (lots of network modifiers) is the leftmost curve; fused silica (pure SiO2 network) is the rightmost, needing the highest temperature. Viscosity falls smoothly by many orders of magnitude: a glass has no sharp melting point.

DEFECTS-10●●○L18 slide 12
Which statement about doping silicon is correct?
Worked solution

Dopants are substitutional point defects. Group 15 (P) has an extra electron → donor level just below the conduction band → n-type. Group 13 (Ga) is one electron short → acceptor level just above the valence band → p-type.

DEFECTS-11●●○L18 slide 17
Explain in 2–3 sentences why (a) 18-carat gold is harder than pure gold and (b) a hammered (wrought) steel is harder than the same steel before.
Worked solution

(a) 18-carat gold has 25% Cu + Ag atoms in the gold lattice (substitutional point defects). These atoms differ slightly in size and make it harder for dislocations to glide past, so the alloy is harder (and stronger) than pure, soft gold.

(b) Hammering creates many new dislocations; they tangle and block each other’s motion (work hardening), so it takes more stress to deform further.

Same idea behind both: anything that blocks dislocation motion makes a metal harder. (Pure iron vs steel: carbon atoms are interstitial defects doing the same job.)

DEFECTS-12●○○L19 slide 3
How does a polycrystalline solid differ from a single crystal?
Worked solution

Inside each grain the atoms are perfectly ordered; neighbouring grains are rotated relative to each other (a grain boundary is a planar defect). Amorphous = no periodic order anywhere.

DEFECTS-13●○○L18
Classify: (i) a vacancy, (ii) an edge dislocation, (iii) a grain boundary.
Worked solution

Defects are classified by dimensionality (L20 concept map): point (0-D: vacancy, interstitial, substitutional), line (1-D: dislocation), planar (2-D: grain boundary).

DEFECTS-14●●○L19 slide 15
High-density polyethylene (HDPE) is called semicrystalline. What does that mean?
Worked solution

Polymer chains are long and tangled, so they cannot all line up; real HDPE has small regions where chains pack periodically (unit cell about 0.741 × 0.494 × 0.255 nm) inside an amorphous tangle.

How far atoms get in time t is about √(Dt). extra The L20 slides print only the first law, the log D plot and the concept map; the Arrhenius form, Fick’s second law and √(Dt) were blank or spoken in lecture, so check your own lecture notes for the exact forms. Typical slips:

  • Q in kJ with R in J.
  • Reading “steeper” as “faster”.
  • Forgetting that depth goes as the square root of time.
DIFFUSION-1●○○L20 concept map
Fick’s first law is J = −D dC/dx. What does the minus sign mean?
Worked solution

dC/dx is negative when concentration falls with distance; the minus sign makes J positive in that direction. The concept map: “Particles move from high concentration to low concentration areas.”

DIFFUSION-2●●●Arrhenius · extra
A solid has activation energy Q = 140 kJ/mol for diffusion. By what factor does D increase when the temperature rises from 1000 K to 1100 K? (R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); D = D0exp(−Q/RT).)
×
Hint
Use Q in J/mol, because R is in J/(mol·K).
Worked solution
  1. D2/D1 = exp[(Q/R)(1/T1 − 1/T2)] (D0 cancels).
  2. Q/R = 140000 / 8.314 = 1.684×104 K. 1/1000 − 1/1100 = 9.09×10−5 K−1.
  3. Exponent = 1.531; D2/D1 = e1.531 = 4.62. A 10% rise in T makes diffusion over 4× faster.
DIFFUSION-3●●○L20 slide 8 · extra
Carbon diffuses into iron with D = 2.0×10−7 cm²/s. Estimate the characteristic distance √(Dt) after 1 hour, in mm.
mm
Worked solution
  1. t = 3600 s. Dt = 2.0×10−7 × 3600 = 7.2×10−4 cm².
  2. √(Dt) = 0.0268 cm = 0.268 mm (1 cm = 10 mm).

Because of the square root, 4× longer only doubles the depth. (L20 slide 8 leaves the “characteristic distance” formula blank; √(Dt) is the standard form.)

DIFFUSION-4●●○L20 slide 5
At the same temperature in iron, which diffuses faster?
Worked solution

The L20 plot: H and C in iron sit far above Fe in Fe by 4–8 orders of magnitude. Small interstitial atoms hop between holes (low activation energy); Fe atoms need a neighbouring vacancy to move (self-diffusion, high Q).

DIFFUSION-5●●○L20 slide 5
On a plot of log D against 1000/T, line A is steeper than line B. Which is true?
Worked solution

Slope = −Q/R. A steeper (more negative) slope means a larger Q: D changes more with temperature. Steeper does not mean faster at every T: the lines can cross. In the plot, 1000/T grows to the right, so hot is on the left.

DIFFUSION-6●●●Arrhenius · extra
A diffusion coefficient is measured as 2.0×10−14 cm²/s at 800 K and 4.0×10−12 cm²/s at 1000 K. Find the activation energy Q in kJ/mol.
kJ/mol
Hint
Same as reading the slope of ln D vs 1/T between two points.
Worked solution
  1. From D = D0e−Q/RT: ln(D2/D1) = (Q/R)(1/T1 − 1/T2).
  2. D2/D1 = 200, ln 200 = 5.298. 1/800 − 1/1000 = 2.5×10−4.
  3. Q = 8.314 × 5.298 / 2.5×10−4 = 1.762×105 J/mol = 176 kJ/mol.
DIFFUSION-7●●○L20 slide 7
When do you need Fick’s second law instead of the steady-state first law?
Worked solution

Steady state: the profile does not change, flux is the same everywhere (first law). Non-steady-state: flux in at x differs from flux out at x + dx, so the concentration builds up or drains with time. The second law (∂C/∂t = D ∂²C/∂x², extra not printed on the slide) relates that change to D.

DIFFUSION-8●○○L20 slide 6
The activation energy for self-diffusion Qv of FCC metals rises with melting point. Which order is right (smallest to largest Qv)?
Worked solution

Stronger bonding (higher melting point) means more energy needed to open a vacancy and hop in: Pb (327 °C) ≈ 25 000 cal/mol, Al ≈ 33 000, Cu ≈ 50 000, Fe (1538 °C) ≈ 68 000 (read from the slide plot, approximate).

DIFFUSION-9●●○L20 slide 11
Atoms diffuse through a thin plate. The concentration is 5.0×1020 atoms/cm³ on one side and 1.0×1020 on the other, 0.20 cm apart, and D = 3.0×10−8 cm²/s. Find the steady-state flux |J| in atoms/(cm²·s).
atoms/(cm²·s)
Worked solution
  1. |dC/dx| = (5.0×1020 − 1.0×1020) / 0.20 cm = 2.0×1021 atoms/cm&sup4;.
  2. |J| = D |dC/dx| = 3.0×10−8 × 2.0×1021 = 6×1013 atoms/(cm²·s), flowing from the high side to the low side.
DIFFUSION-10●●○L20 slide 5–6
L20 asks: “What do we mean by Fe diffusing in Fe?” and “Do you think interstitial diffusion has a higher or lower activation energy than this?” Answer both.
Worked solution

Self-diffusion: the atoms of a pure crystal are identical, but they still move. Imagine a few Fe atoms labelled with a different isotope: over time they spread through the lattice. The mechanism is by vacancies: an atom jumps into a neighbouring empty site. That requires (1) a vacancy to exist and (2) energy to push through the gap, so Q is large (Qv rising with melting point: Fe is about 68 000 cal/mol).

Interstitial diffusion (C or H in Fe) needs no vacancy: the small atom hops between holes. The activation energy is lower, which is why those lines on the L20 plot are shallower and sit orders of magnitude above self-diffusion.
